## 1. It Had Already Started  `b1-it-had-already-started` (grammar, ready)
**Focus:** past perfect for an event that happened before another past event

**had + past participle**
- Use 'had' plus the past participle to form the past perfect.
- The past perfect shows that one past event happened before another past event.
- In this common pattern, the later past event usually uses the past simple.
  - _I had finished my homework before I went out._
  - _She had already left when I arrived._
**already, never, by then + past perfect**
- Words like 'already', 'never', and 'by then' are often used with the past perfect.
- 'Already' shows something happened before another past event.
- 'Never' shows that something did not happen before the other event.
  - _It had already started when I got there._
  - _I had never seen that movie before last night._

## 2. By the Time I Got There  `b1-by-the-time-i-got-there` (grammar, ready)
**Focus:** past perfect with by the time, already and just

**past perfect**
- Use had + past participle to show one past action happened before another.
- The past perfect often comes with time words like by the time, already, and just.
  - _By the time we arrived, the bus had left._
  - _We had already finished dinner when he called._
**by the time**
- By the time shows that something else happened before a past event.
- The past perfect usually goes in the other part of the sentence.
  - _By the time she got to the station, the train had just left._
  - _By the time we noticed, they had already gone._
**already and just**
- Already means something happened before another event, often earlier than expected.
- Just means something happened a short time before another event.
  - _We had already locked the door when she arrived._
  - _He had just left when I called._

## 3. Setting the Scene  `b1-setting-the-scene` (grammar, ready)
**Focus:** past continuous and past simple working together in a story

**past continuous**
- Use was or were plus verb-ing to show an action in progress in the past.
- The subject decides if you use was or were.
  - _I was walking to the store._
  - _They were talking on the phone._
**past simple**
- Use the past form of the verb to show a finished action in the past.
- The past simple often interrupts the past continuous in a story.
  - _I saw my friend._
  - _She called me._
**using both tenses together**
- Use past continuous for the background action and past simple for the action that interrupts.
- You can use 'when' with the past simple and 'while' with the past continuous.
  - _I was walking when I saw an accident._
  - _While we were eating, the phone rang._

## 4. What Had Happened Before  `b1-what-had-happened-before` (grammar, ready)
**Focus:** past perfect in questions and negatives

**Had + subject + past participle?**
- Use 'had' before the subject to make a question in the past perfect.
- The main verb is always in the past participle form.
- Use past perfect questions to ask if something happened before another past event.
  - _Had you ever seen that movie before last night?_
  - _Had they finished dinner when you arrived?_
**subject + had not + past participle**
- Use 'had not' or 'hadn't' before the past participle to make a negative in the past perfect.
- Do not add 'did' or change the past participle.
- Use past perfect negatives to say something did not happen before another past event.
  - _I hadn't heard the news before you told me._
  - _She had not finished her homework when the phone rang._

## 5. Suddenly, Meanwhile, Later  `b1-suddenly-meanwhile-later` (phrases, published)
**Focus:** expressions that move a story from one moment to the next

**all of a sudden**
- Use 'all of a sudden' to show that something happened very quickly and unexpectedly.
- This phrase introduces a surprising or sudden event in the story.
  - _All of a sudden, the bus stopped in the middle of the road._
  - _We were walking, and all of a sudden, it started to rain._
**not long after**
- Use 'not long after' to show that something happened soon after another event.
- This phrase connects two events that are close in time.
  - _Not long after we left, we saw a beautiful mountain._
  - _The train arrived, and not long after, we found our seats._
**in the meantime**
- Use 'in the meantime' for something that happens during the interval before another event or action finishes.
- This phrase often introduces an action that takes place while you are waiting for something else.
  - _We missed the bus and had to wait for the next one. In the meantime, we got some coffee._
  - _The car was being fixed. In the meantime, we walked around the city._

- [say_sentence] we / left the station / we saw a cafe / soon after → Not long after we left the station, we saw a cafe. / We left the station and not long after, we saw a cafe.
- [open] Tell about a time when something surprising happened during a trip. Use at least one of the phrases from this lesson. → örnek: I was traveling by train. All of a sudden, the lights went out. In the meantime, people started talking to each other.

## 8. Have You Finished Yet?  `b1-have-you-finished-yet` (grammar, ready)
**Focus:** choosing between present perfect and past simple by time period

**present perfect for unfinished time**
- Use present perfect for actions in a time period that is not finished (like today, this week, this year).
- Do not use a finished past time with present perfect (not 'yesterday', not 'last week').
  - _Have you finished your homework today?_
  - _I have not seen that movie this week._
**past simple for finished time**
- Use past simple for actions in a finished time period (like yesterday, last week, in 2010).
  - _I did that yesterday._
  - _She called me last night._
**not yet with present perfect**
- Use 'not yet' with present perfect to say something is still not finished in the current time period.
  - _Have you finished your report? Not yet._
  - _I have not finished my lunch yet._

- [fill_blank] ___ your work today? → Have you finished
- [mcq] Which is correct for a finished time: last week? (I have finished that last week. | I did that last week. | Have you finished that last week?) → I did that last week.
- [say_sentence] you / finish / the project / yet? → Have you finished the project yet? / Have you finished the project?

## 64. Enough, Too Much, Plenty  `b1-enough-too-much-plenty` (grammar, ready)
**Focus:** talking about amounts precisely

**not enough**
- 'Not enough' means there is less than what is needed.
- Put 'enough' before a noun: 'enough chairs'. To make the amount negative, use 'not' with the verb: 'We do not have enough chairs.'
- Without a following noun, 'enough' can go after a verb: 'We did not sleep enough.'
- These patterns can describe things, time, people, or activities.
  - _We do not have enough chairs for everyone._
  - _There is not enough water in the bottle._
**too much**
- 'Too much' means there is more than what is wanted or needed.
- Use 'too much' before uncountable nouns.
- 'Too much' can describe things like food, work, or noise.
  - _There is too much sugar in my tea._
  - _You spend too much time on your phone._
**plenty of**
- 'Plenty of' means there is more than enough of something.
- Use 'plenty of' before countable or uncountable nouns.
- 'Plenty of' shows that there is a large, sufficient amount.
  - _We have plenty of time to finish the project._
  - _There are plenty of apples in the basket._

## 68. Telling It Like It Is  `b1-telling-it-like-it-is` (practice, ready)
**Focus:** giving your honest view even when it is uncomfortable

**I have to say**
- Use 'I have to say' to introduce an honest opinion, especially if it may not be positive.

- It shows you are being open and sincere.
  - _I have to say, your report is very detailed._
  - _I have to say, I expected something different._
**if I'm honest**
- Use 'if I'm honest' to show you are telling the truth, even if it is difficult.
- This phrase can make your feedback sound less harsh.
- It signals you are sharing your real thoughts.
  - _If I'm honest, I think the design could be improved._
  - _If I'm honest, this part was a bit confusing._
**no offence, but**
- Use 'no offence, but' to warn that your next words may sound critical.
- It can signal that you do not intend to hurt the other person's feelings, but it does not automatically make criticism polite.
- Even with this phrase, your words can still sound rude, so use it only with people you know well.
  - _No offence, but I think you could do better next time._
  - _No offence, but I do not agree with your idea._
