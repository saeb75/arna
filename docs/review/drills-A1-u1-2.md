# GlotMate — A1 units 1, 2: practice block review

## Instructions for the reviewer

You are reviewing exercise content for **GlotMate**, an app that teaches English to adult beginners through conversation with a tutor avatar. Each lesson has three parts:

1. **Lecture** — the tutor explains the teaching points (shown below as "Teaching points").
2. **Practice block** — tap-based exercises, no keyboard (shown below). Every item has exactly ONE correct answer, graded by code (no AI).
3. **Role play** — free conversation with a character; the character's first line is shown below as "Role play openings" (one per learner context: everyday, work, travel, academic, exam).

**Exercise types:** Fill the gap (choose the word) · Arrange the words (build the sentence from tiles; extra tiles are distractors) · Read the situation, choose · Tap the wrong word · Complete the chat · Match the pairs · Listen and choose (audio plays, choose what you heard) · Listen and arrange · Say this sentence (speech recognition).

**Rules the content must follow:**
- **Exactly one correct answer.** No distractor may also be acceptable in the given context. This is the most important check.
- **Level A1.** Words and grammar a A1 learner can handle; only what the lesson teaches plus very common words.
- **Coverage:** each teaching point (p1, p2…) has at least 3 items; lessons with 2+ points end with at least 2 mixed ("mix") items.
- Distractors should be plausible learner mistakes, not nonsense.
- "Arrange" items: only one correct word order. "Listen and choose": options must sound different enough to tell apart.
- "Say this sentence": no names that speech recognition could misspell.
- "Tap the wrong word": only ONE word can fix the sentence (no subject–verb agreement errors, where changing the subject would also work).
- "Arrange the words": the first tile keeps its capital and the last tile keeps the final . ? ! — so a statement cannot be rebuilt as a question.
- Anything a drill tests (and every role play target phrase) must be taught in the teaching points.
- Role play openings: very simple, end with a question, and invite the learner to use the lesson's target phrases right away. The role play is voice only: an opening may ask about the learner's OWN life or things (they can imagine them), but never about a picture or object only the character can see.

**What to return:** a list of concrete problems only (skip items that are fine). For each: lesson id, the item (quote it), the problem, and your suggested fix. Use these categories: AMBIGUOUS (more than one correct answer), WRONG (the marked answer is not correct), LEVEL (too hard / unnatural for A1), WEAK (works but could teach better), COVERAGE (a teaching point is under-practised), OPENING (role play opening issue). End with any general suggestions.

## 1. Hello, I'm…  `a1-hello-im`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** greeting someone and introducing yourself · **Role play targets:** nice to meet you / my name is / how are you

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — my name is**: Use 'my name is' and then your name to tell someone who you are. 'I'm' plus your name means the same thing and is shorter. _Examples:_ Hello, my name is Daniel. / Hi, I'm Daniel.
- **p2 — nice to meet you**: Say 'nice to meet you' the first time you meet someone. The other person usually answers 'Nice to meet you too.' _Examples:_ Nice to meet you, Sara. / Nice to meet you too.
- **p3 — how are you**: Ask 'How are you?' after hello, with someone you know or someone you have just met. Short answers are 'I'm fine, thanks' or 'Not bad, thanks.' _Examples:_ Hello Sara, how are you? / I'm fine, thanks. And you?

**Practice block (12 items)**

1. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — Hello, my ___ is Leo. → **✔ name** · names · am
2. `p1` _Arrange the words_ — **Hi, my name is Maria.** (extra tiles: I)
3. `p1` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "Hi, I'm Tom." → **✔ Hi, I'm Tom.** · Hi, my name is Tom. · Hello, I'm Leo.
4. `p2` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: Hi, I'm Sara. Nice to meet you. / YOU: ___ → **✔ Nice to meet you too.** · Nice to meet you again. · How old are you?
5. `p2` _Arrange the words_ — **Nice to meet you too.** (extra tiles: two)
6. `p2` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **Nice to meet you.**
7. `p3` _Fill the gap_ — Hello! How ___ you? → **✔ are** · is · am
8. `p3` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: Good morning! How are you? / YOU: ___ → **✔ Not bad, thanks.** · I'm from Spain. · Yes, I am.
9. `p3` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "How are you?" → **✔ How are you?** · How old are you? · Who are you?
10. `p3` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **Hello, how are you?**
11. `mix` _Match the pairs_ — How are you? ↔ I'm fine, thanks. · What's your name? ↔ My name is Leo. · Nice to meet you. ↔ Nice to meet you too.
12. `mix` _Listen and arrange_ — 🔊 **Hello, I'm Leo, nice to meet you.**

**Role play openings**

- everyday: Lucy (a neighbour you have never met) — "Hi! I'm Lucy. I live on the second floor. What's your name?"
- work: David (a new colleague) — "Hi! I'm David. It's my first day here. What's your name?"
- travel: Marta (another traveller at the gate) — "Hi! I'm Marta. What's your name?"
- academic: Tom (a classmate on the first day) — "Hi there! I'm Tom. What's your name?"
- exam: Ms. Green (the examiner) — "Good morning. My name is Ms. Green. What is your name?"

## 2. Am, Is, Are  `a1-am-is-are`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** the present of 'be': affirmative sentences and contractions · **Role play targets:** I am / he is / they are

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — am, is, are**: Use 'am' after I, 'is' after he, she and it, and 'are' after you, we and they. An English sentence needs this verb, so never leave it out. _Examples:_ I am a student. / She is my sister. / They are at home.
- **p2 — I'm, she's, they're**: When you speak, join the two words: I'm, you're, he's, she's, it's, we're, they're. The short form means exactly the same as the long form. _Examples:_ I'm from Spain. / He's my teacher. / We're in the same class.

**Practice block (14 items)**

1. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — I ___ a student. → **✔ am** · is · are
2. `p1` _Match the pairs_ — I ↔ am · she ↔ is · they ↔ are
3. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — My sister ___ a nurse. → **✔ is** · am · are
4. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — We ___ in the same class. → **✔ are** · is · am
5. `p1` _Arrange the words_ — **They are at home.** (extra tiles: is)
6. `p1` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "He is a doctor." → **✔ He is a doctor.** · She is a doctor. · He has a doctor.
7. `p2` _Read the situation, choose_ — What is the short form of 'we are'? → **✔ we're** · were · we's
8. `p2` _Match the pairs_ — I am ↔ I'm · you are ↔ you're · he is ↔ he's · it is ↔ it's
9. `p2` _Tap the wrong word_ — Im from Spain. → wrong word: **Im** → I'm from Spain.
10. `p2` _Listen and arrange_ — 🔊 **We're in the same class.** (extra tiles: were)
11. `p2` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **She's my sister.**
12. `mix` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: Who is that? / YOU: ___ → **✔ She's my teacher.** · She my teacher. · She are my teacher.
13. `mix` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "They're my friends." → **✔ They're my friends.** · They were my friends. · They're your friends.
14. `mix` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **I'm a student and you're a teacher.**

**Role play openings**

## 3. Are You a Student?  `a1-are-you-a-student`

## 4. I'm Not from Here  `a1-im-not-from-here`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** 'be': negative sentences · **Role play targets:** I'm not / it isn't / we aren't

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I'm not / isn't / aren't**: Put 'not' after am, is or are: I am not, she is not, we are not. In speaking, use the short forms: I'm not, she isn't, we aren't. The short form of 'I am not' is 'I'm not' — never 'I amn't'. _Examples:_ I'm not from this city. / She isn't at work today. / We aren't ready yet.
- **p2 — No, but...**: When someone guesses wrong, say the negative first and then the true answer. This sounds friendly and clear at the same time. _Examples:_ I'm not Spanish. I'm Italian. / It isn't my bag. It's her bag.

## 5. Where Are You From?  `a1-where-are-you-from`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** countries and nationalities with 'be from' · **Role play targets:** I'm from / where are you from / I live in

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I'm from**: Use 'I'm from' plus a country or city to say where you come from. Country names in English start with a capital letter: Spain, Japan, Brazil. _Examples:_ I'm from Japan. / She's from a small town near Rome.
- **p2 — Where are you from?**: Ask 'Where are you from?' to find out someone's country or city. The word 'where' comes first, then the verb, then the person. _Examples:_ Where are you from? / Where is your friend from?
- **p3 — I live in**: Use 'I live in' plus a city or country for the place where your home is. You can be from one place and live in another. Ask 'Where do you live?' to find out where someone's home is. _Examples:_ I live in Berlin now. / I'm from Poland, but I live in France. / Where do you live?

**Practice block (11 items)**

1. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — She's ___ a small town near Rome. → **✔ from** · of · to

3. `p1` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **I'm from a small town.**
4. `p2` _Arrange the words_ — **Where are you from?** (extra tiles: do)
5. `p2` _Fill the gap_ — Where ___ you from? → **✔ are** · is · am
6. `p2` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: Hi, I'm Leo. / YOU: ___ / THEM: I'm from Canada. → **✔ Hi Leo! Where are you from?** · Hi Leo! Where you are from? · Hi Leo! Where do you from?

## 6. Meeting Someone New  `a1-meeting-someone-new`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** introducing yourself and asking someone else the same questions · **Role play targets:** nice to meet you / where are you from / what about you

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — What about you?**: After you answer a question, ask 'What about you?' to give the turn back. This short question keeps a conversation going without new words. _Examples:_ I'm from Greece. What about you? / I live near the station. What about you?
- **p2 — Really? / Me too!**: Short reactions like 'Really?' and 'Me too!' show that you are listening. Use 'Me too' when the same thing is true for you. _Examples:_ Really? That's interesting. / Me too! I live there as well.

## 8. He, She, They  `a1-he-she-they`

## 9. Have You Got a Pen?  `a1-have-you-got-a-pen`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** 'have got' for possession: affirmative, negative and questions · **Role play targets:** I've got / have you got / she hasn't got

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I've got / she's got**: Use 'have got' after I, you, we and they, and 'has got' after he, she and it. In speaking, join the words: I've got, you've got, she's got. _Examples:_ I've got two brothers. / She's got a new phone.
- **p2 — Have you got...?**: Put 'have' or 'has' first to make a question: 'Have you got a pen?' Answer with 'Yes, I have' or 'No, I haven't'. _Examples:_ Have you got a pen? / Yes, I have. / Has she got the keys?
- **p3 — haven't got / hasn't got**: For negatives, put 'not' after have or has: I haven't got, she hasn't got. _Examples:_ I haven't got any money with me. / He hasn't got a car.

**Practice block (13 items)**

1. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — She ___ got a new bike. → **✔ has** · have · is

## 10. What's Your Job?  `a1-whats-your-job`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** talking about jobs, with a / an before professions · **Role play targets:** what do you do / I work as a / he's an engineer

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I'm a teacher**: When you say one person's job with I'm, he's or she's, use 'a' or 'an': 'I'm a teacher', not 'I'm teacher'. Use 'an' when the job starts with a vowel sound: an engineer, an artist, an accountant. You can also say 'I work as a' plus the job: 'I work as a nurse.' _Examples:_ I'm a nurse. / He's an engineer. / I work as a designer.
- **p2 — What do you do?**: 'What do you do?' is the normal way to ask about someone's job. Learn it as one whole phrase; it does not mean 'what are you doing now'. _Examples:_ What do you do? / I'm a designer. What do you do?

## 11. How Old Are You?  `a1-how-old-are-you`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** numbers up to one hundred and asking about age · **Role play targets:** how old are you / how old is / years old

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — How old are you?**: Ask about age with 'How old are you?' and answer with 'I'm' plus the number. Do not add the word 'years' unless you say 'years old': 'I'm thirty' or 'I'm thirty years old'. _Examples:_ How old are you? / I'm twenty-five years old. / She's ten years old.
- **p2 — numbers up to one hundred**: The tens are twenty, thirty, forty, fifty, sixty, seventy, eighty, ninety, and then one hundred. ## 12. People in My Life  `a1-people-in-my-life`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** talking about your family, friends and the people you work with · **Role play targets:** this is my / we're good friends / she's really nice

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — we're good friends**: Use 'we're' plus a phrase to describe a relationship: we're good friends, we're neighbours. Add one detail after it, so the other person can ask more. _Examples:_ We're good friends from school. / We're neighbours and we see each other every day.
