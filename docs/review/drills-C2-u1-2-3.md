## 1. The Weight of a Word  `c2-the-weight-of-a-word`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** choosing between near-synonyms by what they suggest, not what they mean · **Role play targets:** I'd hesitate to call / more accurate to say / that carries a suggestion

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I'd hesitate to call**: 'I'd hesitate to call it X' declines a word while leaving the underlying point standing. It objects to the label, so it fails if your disagreement is really with the substance. _Examples:_ I'd hesitate to call it a failure. / I'd hesitate to call that generous.
- **p2 — more accurate to say**: 'It's more accurate to say Y' offers the replacement instead of only rejecting the first word. 'More accurate' claims a better fit rather than the only right word, which is why it sounds precise rather than pedantic. _Examples:_ It's more accurate to say that the project stalled. / It would be more accurate to describe her as cautious.
- **p3 — that carries a suggestion**: 'That carries a suggestion of X' names the connotation rather than the meaning. Two words can describe the same behaviour and carry opposite suggestions: 'thrifty' praises it, 'stingy' criticises it. _Examples:_ That carries a suggestion of blame. / 'Cheap' carries a suggestion of poor quality as well as low price.

**Practice block (11 items)**

1. `p1` _Read the situation, choose_ — A colleague calls your project a failure. You accept that it had problems, but you reject that word. What do you say? → **✔ I'd hesitate to call it a failure.** · Yes, it was a complete failure. · There were no problems at all.
2. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — I'd hesitate to ___ it a failure. → **✔ call** · calling · called
3. `p1` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **I'd hesitate to call it a mistake.**
4. `p2` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: You said the project failed. / YOU: Well, not exactly. / THEM: So what happened, then? / YOU: ___ → **✔ It's more accurate to say it stalled.** · It's more accurate to say it failed. · It's less accurate to say it stalled.
5. `p2` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "It would be more accurate to call him careful." → **✔ It would be more accurate to call him careful.** · It would be more polite to ask him first. · She would describe it as a careful plan.
6. `p2` _Listen and arrange_ — 🔊 **It's more accurate to say it stalled.**
7. `p3` _Read the situation, choose_ — 'Thrifty' and 'stingy' can both describe someone who spends little money. You want to point out that 'stingy' sounds like criticism. What do you say? → **✔ 'Stingy' carries a suggestion of meanness.** · 'Stingy' carries a suggestion of praise. · 'Stingy' suggests exactly what 'thrifty' suggests.
8. `p3` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "That carries a suggestion of blame." → **✔ That carries a suggestion of blame.** · That carries a lot of weight with me. · They suggested we should leave early.
9. `p3` _Fill the gap_ — 'Cheap' carries a ___ of poor quality. → **✔ suggestion** · suggest · suggested
10. `mix` _Match the pairs_ — I'd hesitate to call it X. ↔ rejects a label · It's more accurate to say Y. ↔ offers a better word · That carries a suggestion of Z. ↔ names what a word implies
11. `mix` _Read the situation, choose_ — Someone calls your plan 'risky'. You reject that word and offer 'ambitious' instead. What do you say? → **✔ I'd hesitate to call it risky. It's more accurate to say it's ambitious.** · I'd hesitate to call it ambitious. It's more accurate to say it's risky. · I'd call it risky. It's more accurate to say it's reckless.

**Role play openings**

- everyday: Marisol (a friend being blunt about your work) — "So the whole thing was a failure, then?"
- work: Mr. Halloran (a colleague drafting a report) — "I wrote that the rollout was 'chaotic'. Fair word?"
- travel: Ngozi (a fellow traveller describing a place) — "I'd call that town 'shabby'. Would you?"
- academic: Professor Lindqvist (your tutor) — "In your essay you called the plan 'reckless'. Is that really the word you want?"
- exam: Ms. Ferrante (the examiner) — "Would you describe your generation as 'entitled'?"

## 2. Faint Praise  `c2-faint-praise`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** saying something positive that is heard as criticism · **Role play targets:** it's certainly different / not bad for / you've clearly worked hard

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — it's certainly different**: 'It's certainly different' comments on distinctiveness while withholding any positive evaluation; in context that omission can be heard as criticism. Between friends it is a shared joke; to someone who wanted your approval it reads as a verdict. _Examples:_ Well, it's certainly different. / It's certainly different from the last one.
- **p2 — not bad for**: 'Not bad for a first attempt' praises against a lowered standard, and the standard is what is heard. The 'for' clause is the damage: remove it and the same sentence is a compliment. _Examples:_ Not bad for a first attempt. / Not bad for a Monday.
- **p3 — you've clearly worked hard**: Praising effort alone can imply you have little to say about the result; a direct comment on the result removes that implication. It is safest where effort was the point — a learner, a volunteer — and riskiest where the result was. _Examples:_ You've clearly worked hard on this. / You've clearly worked hard — it shows.

**Practice block (11 items)**

1. `p1` _Read the situation, choose_ — A close friend bought a very strange hat as a joke. You want to tease them without saying you like it and without insulting them directly. What do you say? → **✔ It's certainly different, I'll give you that.** · It genuinely looks wonderful on you. · I hate it, and you should take it back.
2. `p1` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "It's certainly different from the last one." → **✔ It's certainly different from the last one.** · Well, it certainly made a difference. · Well, it's certainly the right one.
3. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — Your new haircut is ___ different. → **✔ certainly** · certain · certainty
4. `p2` _Read the situation, choose_ — A colleague's first presentation was weak. You want to say something positive that still signals it was weak. What do you say? → **✔ Not bad for a first attempt.** · That was truly excellent. · That was a complete disaster.
5. `p2` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: How was the new restaurant? / YOU: The food was average, but it was their first day, so I don't want to be harsh. / THEM: So, your verdict? / YOU: ___ → **✔ Not bad for a first day.** · It was the worst meal of my life. · Not bad for a last day.
6. `p2` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **Not bad for a first attempt.**
7. `p3` _Read the situation, choose_ — A new volunteer spent all weekend on a poster. The design is weak, but the effort is what matters here. What do you say? → **✔ You've clearly worked hard on this.** · You clearly didn't try at all. · The design is weak, I'm afraid.
8. `p3` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "You've clearly worked hard on this report." → **✔ You've clearly worked hard on this report.** · You clearly work too hard these days. · You should work harder on this.
9. `p3` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: My director looked at my report and only said, 'You've clearly worked hard.' Was that a compliment? / YOU: ___ → **✔ Maybe not. Praising only the effort can mean the result was weak.** · Yes. It means the result was excellent. · No. It means you worked too little.
10. `mix` _Match the pairs_ — It's certainly different. ↔ comments only on being unusual · Not bad for a beginner. ↔ sets a lower standard · You've clearly worked hard. ↔ praises effort, not the result
11. `mix` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "It's certainly different, and not bad for a first try." → **✔ It's certainly different, and not bad for a first try.** · It's certainly the best thing you've written. · It's different from the first one I tried.

**Role play openings**

- everyday: Yevgenia (a close friend who enjoys teasing) — "Imagine my first painting: a purple cow on a beach. Be honest, what would you say about it?"
- work: Mr. Dunne (a colleague showing you his new slides) — "Imagine my slides are all bright pink, with music on each one. What would you tell me?"
- travel: Amara (a fellow guest at a dinner) — "That main course was fish in chocolate sauce. What did you make of it?"
- academic: Dr. Ferreira (your tutor) — "A classmate's first essay is weak but very unusual. What would you say to them?"
- exam: Ms. Whitmore (the examiner) — "Imagine a friend's first story is about a toaster that falls in love. What would you say to them?"

## 3. Deliberate Vagueness  `c2-deliberate-vagueness`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** being unspecific on purpose while still sounding responsive · **Role play targets:** something along those lines / one might say / in a manner of speaking

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — something along those lines**: A vague nominal like 'something along those lines' confirms the shape of an answer without confirming the detail. It is honest when the detail genuinely is not fixed, and misleading when it is fixed and you are hiding it. _Examples:_ Yes, something along those lines. / A summer launch, or something along those lines.
- **p2 — one might say**: 'One might say' presents a formulation tentatively and at some distance; it does not necessarily mean you refuse to endorse it. Overused it sounds evasive rather than careful, because the listener notices you never speak in your own voice. _Examples:_ One might say the timing was unfortunate. / One might say it was always unlikely.
- **p3 — in a manner of speaking**: 'In a manner of speaking' concedes a claim loosely while flagging that it is not exact. The flag is what keeps it honest: without it the listener may take the loose claim more precisely than you meant. _Examples:_ Yes, in a manner of speaking. / We agreed, in a manner of speaking.

**Practice block (11 items)**

1. `p1` _Read the situation, choose_ — A reporter asks if the launch is in June. It is planned for early summer, but the exact date is not fixed. What do you say? → **✔ Early summer, or something along those lines.** · Yes, the launch is on the third of June. · I can't tell you anything at all.
2. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — A summer launch, or something ___ those lines. → **✔ along** · among · across
3. `p1` _Listen and arrange_ — 🔊 **We expect a delay, or something along those lines.**
4. `p2` _Read the situation, choose_ — Your manager asks what went wrong. You want to suggest the timing was bad, but say it tentatively and impersonally. What do you say? → **✔ One might say the timing was unfortunate.** · The timing was a total disaster, full stop. · I blame you for the timing.
5. `p2` _Fill the gap_ — One might ___ the timing was unfortunate. → **✔ say** · says · saying
6. `p2` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **One might say it was always unlikely.**
7. `p3` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: I hear you and your partner only talked and nothing is signed. But you agreed, right? / YOU: ___ → **✔ In a manner of speaking, yes.** · Yes, we signed every detail yesterday. · No, we have never spoken.
8. `p3` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "We agreed, in a manner of speaking." → **✔ We agreed, in a manner of speaking.** · We agreed on the manner of payment. · We agreed to speak in a calm voice.
9. `p3` _Read the situation, choose_ — You loosely agree that the meeting 'went well', but you want the listener to know this is not exact. What do you say? → **✔ Yes, in a manner of speaking.** · Yes, exactly, in every way. · No, the meeting never happened.
10. `mix` _Match the pairs_ — something along those lines ↔ confirms the shape, not the detail · one might say ↔ speaks impersonally, not as 'I' · in a manner of speaking ↔ flags a claim as loose
11. `mix` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "One might say we agreed, in a manner of speaking." → **✔ One might say we agreed, in a manner of speaking.** · One day we might agree on the manner. · Someone said we agreed to speak.

**Role play openings**

- everyday: Rosalind (a friend pressing for details) — "Come on, you must know the date by now?"
- work: Mr. Achterberg (a journalist on a call) — "Can you confirm a launch date for me?"
- travel: Leyla (a fellow passenger asking about your plans) — "So how long are you staying?"
- academic: Professor Adeyinka (your tutor) — "Your thesis results are not final yet. Can you tell me what you found?"
- exam: Mr. Sanderson (the examiner) — "You don't track it exactly, but roughly how many hours a week do you study?"

## 4. The Art of Understatement  `c2-the-art-of-understatement`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** dialling a description down so the gap does the work · **Role play targets:** slightly inconvenient / something of an issue / to put it mildly

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — slightly inconvenient**: Calling a serious problem 'slightly inconvenient' works because the gap is obvious to both of you. If the listener does not know how bad it is, the same words are simply inaccurate. _Examples:_ The fire was slightly inconvenient. / Losing the data was slightly inconvenient.
- **p2 — something of an issue**: 'Something of an issue' understates while still marking the thing as a problem. A listener who does not share this habit of understatement may take it literally and miss how serious the problem is. _Examples:_ The budget is something of an issue. / That is something of an issue for us.
- **p3 — to put it mildly**: 'To put it mildly' explicitly signals that you are understating, so the gap is not left to chance. It is the safer form, though the listener may still need context to gauge the real scale. _Examples:_ It was expensive, to put it mildly. / He was annoyed, to put it mildly.

**Practice block (11 items)**

1. `p1` _Read the situation, choose_ — Your friend knows your flight was cancelled and you slept at the airport. You joke about it with deliberate understatement. What do you say? → **✔ It was slightly inconvenient.** · It was a total nightmare from start to finish. · It was slightly convenient.
2. `p1` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "The fire was slightly inconvenient." → **✔ The fire was slightly inconvenient.** · The flight was slightly delayed. · The fire was put out quickly.
3. `p1` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **Losing the data was slightly inconvenient.**
4. `p2` _Fill the gap_ — Staffing is something ___ an issue. → **✔ of** · for · in
5. `p2` _Read the situation, choose_ — Your team has run out of money for the project. In a calm meeting, you mention it while still marking it as a problem. What do you say? → **✔ Money is something of an issue right now.** · Money is no issue at all right now. · Money is something for an issue right now.
6. `p2` _Listen and arrange_ — 🔊 **Parking is something of an issue here.**
7. `p3` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: How much did the repairs cost? / YOU: ___ → **✔ A lot, to put it mildly.** · A lot, to put it mild. · A lot, to putting it mildly.
8. `p3` _Read the situation, choose_ — Your manager asks how the client reacted. The client was furious, and your manager knows nothing yet. You speak mildly but make clear it was worse. What do you say? → **✔ They were unhappy, to put it mildly.** · They were unhappy, but only a little. · They were delighted, to put it mildly.
9. `p3` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "He was annoyed, to put it mildly." → **✔ He was annoyed, to put it mildly.** · He was annoyed and put it away. · He was mild and easy to talk to.
10. `mix` _Read the situation, choose_ — Your landlord ended your lease with one week's notice. A stranger asks about your week and knows nothing. Which answer shows them it is serious? → **✔ Finding a new flat this week is something of an issue, to put it mildly.** · Finding a new flat this week is slightly inconvenient. · Finding a new flat this week is no issue at all.
11. `mix` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "It was something of an issue, to put it mildly." → **✔ It was something of an issue, to put it mildly.** · It was an issue of some kind, I must admit. · It was mild, so it was not an issue.

**Role play openings**

- everyday: Domenico (a friend who knows what happened) — "I heard your flat flooded on the day you moved in. How was the great disaster, then?"
- work: Ms. Iyer (a colleague being briefed) — "I heard the client lost all their data overnight. Give me the short version: how bad is it?"
- travel: Tobias (a stranger on a delayed train) — "This train is four hours late. Have you been waiting long?"
- academic: Dr. Marchetti (your tutor) — "Your experiment caught fire last week. How would you describe it to the class?"
- exam: Ms. Okonjo (the examiner) — "Tell me about something that went wrong recently?"

## 5. Describing a Person Precisely  `c2-describing-a-person-precisely`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** building a portrait of someone that avoids cliche · **Role play targets:** what defines them is / they come across as / I'd describe them as

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — what defines them is**: 'What defines them is X' commits to one central quality instead of listing several. Choosing one is the difficulty; a generic list of adjectives rarely tells the listener which quality matters most. _Examples:_ What defines them is patience. / What defines them is how little they need to say.
- **p2 — they come across as**: 'They come across as X' reports the impression they give, not a claim about their character. The distinction matters because impressions are often wrong, and you may need to say so later. _Examples:_ They come across as cold at first. / They come across as certain, though they aren't.
- **p3 — I'd describe them as**: 'I'd describe them as X' marks the description as yours rather than as established fact. Owning the description makes a strong adjective usable without it becoming a verdict. _Examples:_ I'd describe them as relentless. / I'd describe them as generous with their time.

**Practice block (11 items)**

1. `p1` _Read the situation, choose_ — A friend asks what your new manager is like. You want to name one central quality, not a list. What do you say? → **✔ What defines her is patience.** · She's nice, clever, funny and kind. · What defines her is nice, clever and kind.
2. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — What ___ him is how little he needs to say. → **✔ defines** · define · defining
3. `p1` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **What defines her is how carefully she listens.**
4. `p2` _Read the situation, choose_ — A new colleague seems cold in meetings, but you have not decided what he is really like. How do you describe this? → **✔ He comes across as cold.** · He is a cold person, and that's a fact. · He comes across as warm.
5. `p2` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: Is your neighbour as confident as she seems? / YOU: Not at all. She's very nervous inside. / THEM: So how would you describe her? / YOU: ___ → **✔ She comes across as confident, though she isn't.** · She comes across as nervous, and she is. · She is truly confident, through and through.
6. `p2` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "They come across as shy at first." → **✔ They come across as shy at first.** · They came back cold and tired. · They come to us at first light.
7. `p3` _Read the situation, choose_ — You want to call your boss 'relentless', but make clear it is your own view, not a fact. What do you say? → **✔ I'd describe her as relentless.** · Everyone knows she's relentless. · She is relentless, and that's a fact.
8. `p3` _Listen and arrange_ — 🔊 **I'd describe him as generous with his time.**
9. `p3` _Fill the gap_ — I'd describe them ___ quietly attentive. → **✔ as** · like · for
10. `mix` _Read the situation, choose_ — Your friend's core quality is loyalty. People think she is shy, but in your view she is calm. Which description fits? → **✔ What defines her is loyalty. She comes across as shy, but I'd describe her as calm.** · What defines her is shyness. She comes across as loyal, but I'd describe her as calm. · What defines her is calm. She comes across as loyal, but I'd describe her as shy.
11. `mix` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "They come across as reserved, but I'd describe them as kind." → **✔ They come across as reserved, but I'd describe them as kind.** · They came across a reserved table for us. · I'd describe the room as kind of small.

**Role play openings**

- everyday: Constance (a friend who has not met the person) — "You keep mentioning him. What is he actually like?"
- work: Mr. Bramwell (a colleague hiring for your team) — "You have worked with her. How would you describe her?"
- travel: Paloma (a travelling companion) — "I found him hard to read. Did you?"

## 6. The Same Fact, Three Framings  `c2-the-same-fact-three-framings`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** reframing one event three ways without changing the facts · **Role play targets:** put another way / seen differently / or you could say

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — put another way**: 'Put another way' signals a restatement, so the listener does not hear a new claim. If the restatement adds a fact it is not a reframing, and the signal becomes misleading. _Examples:_ Put another way, we chose caution. / Put another way, nobody was ready.
- **p2 — seen differently**: 'Seen differently' changes the vantage point rather than the content. It works when the second view is genuinely available from the same facts. _Examples:_ Seen differently, that was a lucky escape. / Seen differently, the delay saved us.
- **p3 — or you could say**: 'Or you could say' offers the alternative framing without abandoning your own. Holding both is the point here: if you drop the first framing, it can sound like a retraction. _Examples:_ Or you could say we were cautious. / Or you could say it was never viable.

**Practice block (11 items)**

1. `p1` _Read the situation, choose_ — You said, 'We waited until the risks were clear.' Now you restate the same fact in other words, adding nothing new. What do you say? → **✔ Put another way, we chose caution.** · Put another way, we also lost money. · Put another way, we were never cautious.
2. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — Put another ___, we ran out of time. → **✔ way** · time · word
3. `p1` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **Put another way, we ran out of time.**
4. `p2` _Read the situation, choose_ — The train was cancelled, so you missed a meeting that turned out to be a disaster. You present the same facts from a positive angle. What do you say? → **✔ Seen differently, the cancellation saved me from a disastrous meeting.** · Seen differently, the meeting was a success. · Seen differently, the train was on time.
5. `p2` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "Seen differently, that was a lucky escape." → **✔ Seen differently, that was a lucky escape.** · Seen from here, it looks a long way down. · She did it differently, and she escaped.
6. `p2` _Listen and arrange_ — 🔊 **Seen differently, the delay saved us.**
7. `p3` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: So you think the plan was too risky? / YOU: I do. The risk was high and the possible profit was small. / THEM: Is there another way to see it? / YOU: ___ → **✔ Or you could say the profit didn't justify the risk.** · Or you could say it was never risky. · No, I've changed my mind. It was safe.
8. `p3` _Read the situation, choose_ — You called the result 'cautious'. You want to offer a second framing but keep your own. What do you say? → **✔ Or you could say we were slow.** · Forget what I said. We were slow. · I was wrong to call it cautious.
9. `p3` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "Or you could say we were cautious." → **✔ Or you could say we were cautious.** · We could go, or you could stay here. · You said we would be cautious.
10. `mix` _Read the situation, choose_ — Facts: the project was late but under budget. You give one restatement and one alternative view, adding no facts. Which works? → **✔ Put another way, we missed the deadline. Seen differently, we spent less than planned.** · Put another way, we finished early. Seen differently, we spent less than planned. · Put another way, we missed the deadline. Seen differently, we spent more than planned.
11. `mix` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "Put another way, we were cautious; seen differently, we were slow." → **✔ Put another way, we were cautious; seen differently, we were slow.** · Put it away, we were cautious and slow. · Seen from the other side, we were fast.

**Role play openings**

- everyday: Ivo (a friend hearing your version) — "I heard your team's launch was six months late. Was it a disaster?"
- work: Ms. Delgado (a colleague preparing a review) — "The launch was six months late but under budget. How do we put that to the board?"
- travel: Hakan (a fellow traveller comparing experiences) — "That was the worst holiday I have had. You disagree?"

## 7. Choosing Your Words Carefully  `c2-choosing-your-words-carefully`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** a conversation where the exact wording carries real consequences · **Role play targets:** I want to be careful / let me rephrase / what I mean precisely

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I want to be careful**: 'I want to be careful here' tells the listener the caution is deliberate, not evasion. Without the flag, slow careful speech is often read as hiding something. _Examples:_ I want to be careful how I put this. / I want to be careful not to overstate it.
- **p2 — let me rephrase**: 'Let me rephrase that' withdraws your own wording without withdrawing the point. Correcting yourself openly costs less standing than leaving a bad formulation on the record. _Examples:_ Let me rephrase that. / Let me rephrase — I put that badly.
- **p3 — what I mean precisely**: 'What I mean precisely is X' replaces an approximation with the exact claim. It commits you, so it only helps if the precise version is one you will stand behind. _Examples:_ What I mean precisely is that we cannot verify it. / What I mean precisely is nobody signed it.

**Practice block (11 items)**

1. `p1` _Read the situation, choose_ — A lawyer asks if you knew about the fault. You will answer slowly, and you want them to see the caution is deliberate, not evasion. How do you begin? → **✔ I want to be careful how I say this.** · I don't want to answer that. · I want to be quick about this.
2. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — I want to be careful not ___ overstate it. → **✔ to** · for · of
3. `p1` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "I want to be careful how I say this." → **✔ I want to be careful how I say this.** · I want you to carry this for me. · I wanted to put it in the car.

## 8. Appeals That Work  `c2-appeals-that-work`

## 9. The Rhetorical Question  `c2-the-rhetorical-question`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** questions that make a statement rather than seek an answer · **Role play targets:** who wouldn't want / isn't that the point / why would anyone

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — who wouldn't want**: 'Who wouldn't want X?' assumes agreement, so disagreeing means putting yourself outside 'everyone'. It fails where a reasonable person would answer 'I wouldn't', which then hands them the point. _Examples:_ Who wouldn't want a shorter week? / Who wouldn't want to be told the truth?
- **p2 — isn't that the point**: 'Isn't that the point?' turns an objection into support for your own case. It only works if the objection genuinely serves you; forced, it sounds like a trick. _Examples:_ Isn't that the point of a trial? / Isn't that exactly the point?
- **p3 — why would anyone**: 'Why would anyone do that?' states that no reason exists without you having to prove it. The stronger the listener's actual reason, the worse this lands, because they will supply it. _Examples:_ Why would anyone agree to that? / Why would anyone risk it twice?

4. `p2` _Read the situation, choose_ — Your manager objects: 'But a trial might fail.' Finding out what fails is exactly why you run a trial. What do you say? → **✔ Isn't that the whole point of trying it?** · Yes, so let's cancel the trial. · Why would anyone run a trial?
5. `p2` _Fill the gap_ — ___ that exactly the point? → **✔ Isn't** · Doesn't · Aren't
6. `p2` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "Isn't that the whole point of testing?" → **✔ Isn't that the whole point of testing?** · Isn't this the last day of the trial? · That was the point I tried to make.
7. `p3` _Read the situation, choose_ — Someone suggests signing a contract nobody has read. You want to say there is no reason to do this, using a question. What do you say? → **✔ Why would anyone sign that without reading it?** · Who wouldn't want to sign that? · Would you like to sign that now?

## 10. Repetition and Rhythm  `c2-repetition-and-rhythm`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** patterns of repetition that make a point stick · **Role play targets:** again and again / not once but twice / over and over

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — again and again**: 'Again and again' makes a pattern audible where a number would only be counted. If the listener knows it happened only twice, it sounds exaggerated and costs you credibility. _Examples:_ We raised it again and again. / The same fault, again and again.
- **p2 — not once but twice**: 'Not once but twice' gets its force from the exact number, so it must be exact. It is the precise alternative to 'again and again' and works where the count is small. _Examples:_ Not once but twice this month. / They missed it not once but twice.
- **p3 — over and over**: 'Over and over' emphasises the wearing effect rather than the count. Further repetition can build rhythm and force, but without variation or progression it becomes monotonous. _Examples:_ We explained it over and over. / The same question, over and over.

## 14. Changing Someone's Mind  `c2-changing-someones-mind`

- **p1 — what would convince you**: 'What would convince you?' asks what evidence would change their mind, which tests whether any would. The answer tells you whether to argue evidence or to stop arguing. _Examples:_ What would convince you otherwise? / What would convince you I'm right?

## 15. Opening Positions  `c2-opening-positions`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** the language of a first offer and a first response · **Role play targets:** what we're looking for / our starting point / ideally we'd want

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — what we're looking for**: 'What we're looking for is X' states a position as a goal rather than as a demand. A goal invites a counter-offer; a demand invites a yes or a no. _Examples:_ What we're looking for is a three-year term. / What we're looking for is simple.
- **p2 — our starting point**: 'Our starting point is X' marks the figure as an opening rather than a limit; movement from it can still carry a cost. It also tells the other side to expect movement, including from them. _Examples:_ Our starting point is forty. / That is our starting point on volume.
- **p3 — ideally we'd want**: 'Ideally we'd want X' names your best case while conceding it is the best case. It reveals your preference, which is the cost you pay to speed the negotiation up. _Examples:_ Ideally we'd want it by June. / Ideally we'd want both, realistically one.

3. `p1` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **What we're looking for is a five-year contract.**
4. `p2` _Read the situation, choose_ — You name forty units as your first figure, but you want it heard as an opening, not your limit. What do you say? → **✔ Our starting point is forty.** · Forty is our final offer. · We can't go above or below forty.
5. `p2` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: What's your figure? / YOU: Forty units, but we have room to move. / THEM: So forty is your absolute limit? / YOU: ___ → **✔ No, that's our starting point.** · Yes, that's our starting point. · No, that's our final point.
6. `p2` _Fill the gap_ — Our ___ point is forty. → **✔ starting** · started · starts

## 16. Trading and Conceding  `c2-trading-and-conceding`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** giving something in order to get something · **Role play targets:** if you can do / we could move on / in exchange for

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — if you can do**: 'If you can do X, we can do Y' makes the concession conditional in the same breath. A concession offered first and conditioned afterwards has already been banked by the other side. _Examples:_ If you can do June, we can do the higher volume. / If you can do that, we have a deal.
- **p2 — we could move on**: 'We could move on price' puts price in play; add 'not on scope' if you want the other items to stay fixed. 'Could' keeps the movement hypothetical until you get something in return. Name the item: without it, 'we could move on' means 'let's go to the next topic'. _Examples:_ We could move on price. / We could move on timing, not on scope.
- **p3 — in exchange for**: 'In exchange for X' states the trade openly, which makes reneging visible later. Naming the exchange also stops a concession being remembered as a gift. _Examples:_ We could accept the later date in exchange for a longer term. / We can reduce the price in exchange for earlier payment.

**Practice block (11 items)**

1. `p1` _Read the situation, choose_ — A client wants a lower price. You will only lower it if they sign for three years, and you say both in one sentence. What do you say? → **✔ If you can do three years, we can do the lower price.** · OK, we'll lower the price. Maybe you could sign for three years? · We can't lower the price at all.
2. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — If you can ___ June, we can do the higher volume. → **✔ do** · does · doing
3. `p1` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **If you can do that, we have a deal.**
4. `p2` _Read the situation, choose_ — You are willing to change the price, but not the scope. You name the item in play. What do you say? → **✔ We could move on price, not on scope.** · We could move on scope, not on price. · We can't move on anything.
5. `p2` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: Is the delivery date fixed? / YOU: Not completely. / THEM: So what are you saying? / YOU: ___ → **✔ We could move on timing, if you can do a longer term.** · Timing is completely fixed. · We could move on to lunch now.
6. `p2` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "We could move on timing, not on scope." → **✔ We could move on timing, not on scope.** · We could move into town next spring. · We could talk about timing at noon.
7. `p3` _Read the situation, choose_ — You accept a later date, and you want a longer contract in return. You state the trade openly. What do you say? → **✔ We can accept the later date in exchange for a longer term.** · We can accept the later date as a gift. · We can't accept the later date.
8. `p3` _Fill the gap_ — We'll waive the fee in exchange ___ a longer contract. → **✔ for** · of · to
9. `p3` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: Can you give us free delivery? / YOU: ___ → **✔ Yes, in exchange for a bigger order.** · Yes, in exchange of a bigger order. · Yes, exchange for a bigger order.
10. `mix` _Read the situation, choose_ — You can move on timing if the client signs for three years. You name the item and the trade. Which fits? → **✔ We could move on timing in exchange for a three-year term.** · We could move on timing, and we ask for nothing. · In exchange for timing, we could move on.
11. `mix` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "If you can do June, we could move on price." → **✔ If you can do June, we could move on price.** · If you can come in June, we could talk. · If you can't do June, we can't move.

**Role play openings**

- everyday: Severina (a private buyer) — "Knock fifty off the bike and I will take it today. Deal?"

## 17. Deadlock and Deadlines  `c2-deadlock-and-deadlines`

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — we're going round in circles**: 'We're going round in circles' names the deadlock as shared rather than as their fault. - **p2 — by end of week**: A named deadline converts an open disagreement into a decision with a date; 'by end of week' is business shorthand for 'by the end of the week'. - **p3 — let's park that**: 'Let's park that' sets one blocking item aside so the rest can progress. Parked items must be picked up again; a park that is really a drop will be noticed. _Examples:_ Let's park that and come back to it. / Let's park the payment clause.

## 19. A Difficult Client  `c2-a-difficult-client`

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I understand the frustration**: 'I understand the frustration' acknowledges the feeling without agreeing with the demand. ## 21. Walking Away  `c2-walking-away`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** ending without a deal but keeping the relationship intact · **Role play targets:** I don't think we / no hard feelings / let's stay in touch

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I don't think we**: 'I don't think we can get there' declines the deal rather than the person.
