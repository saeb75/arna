## Instructions for the reviewer

You are reviewing exercise content for **GlotMate**, an app that teaches English to adult beginners through conversation with a tutor avatar. Each lesson has three parts:

1. **Lecture** — the tutor explains the teaching points (shown below as "Teaching points").
2. **Practice block** — tap-based exercises, no keyboard (shown below). Every item has exactly ONE correct answer, graded by code (no AI).
3. **Role play** — free conversation with a character; the character's first line is shown below as "Role play openings" (one per learner context: everyday, work, travel, academic, exam).

**Exercise types:** Fill the gap (choose the word) · Arrange the words (build the sentence from tiles; extra tiles are distractors) · Read the situation, choose · Tap the wrong word · Complete the chat · Match the pairs · Listen and choose (audio plays, choose what you heard) · Listen and arrange · Say this sentence (speech recognition).

**Rules the content must follow:**
- **Exactly one correct answer.** No distractor may also be acceptable in the given context. This is the most important check.
- **Level B1.** Words and grammar a B1 learner can handle; only what the lesson teaches plus very common words.
- **Coverage:** each teaching point (p1, p2…) has at least 3 items; lessons with 2+ points end with at least 2 mixed ("mix") items.
- Distractors should be plausible learner mistakes, not nonsense.
- "Arrange" items: only one correct word order. "Listen and choose": options must sound different enough to tell apart.
- "Say this sentence": no names that speech recognition could misspell.
- "Tap the wrong word": only ONE word can fix the sentence (no subject–verb agreement errors, where changing the subject would also work).
- "Arrange the words": the first tile keeps its capital and the last tile keeps the final . ? ! — so a statement cannot be rebuilt as a question.
- Anything a drill tests (and every role play target phrase) must be taught in the teaching points.
- Role play openings: very simple, end with a question, and invite the learner to use the lesson's target phrases right away. The role play is voice only: an opening may ask about the learner's OWN life or things (they can imagine them), but never about a picture or object only the character can see.

## 19. If I Had More Time  `b1-if-i-had-more-time`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** second conditional for unreal or unlikely present situations · **Role play targets:** if I had / I would / I'd probably

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — if + past simple, would + base verb**: Use the second conditional to talk about unreal or unlikely situations in the present. The structure is: if + past simple, would + base verb. The order can be changed: 'If I had time, I would travel.' or 'I would travel if I had time.' _Examples:_ If I had a million dollars, I would buy a big house. / I would travel the world if I had more free time.
- **p2 — I'd probably**: 'I'd' is the contraction of 'I would'. Use 'I'd probably' to show something is likely in your imaginary situation. _Examples:_ If I had a different job, I'd probably be happier. / I'd probably move to the beach if I had the chance.

**Practice block (12 items)**

## 20. Unless You Ask  `b1-unless-you-ask`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** unless, as long as and provided that for stating conditions · **Role play targets:** unless you / as long as / provided that you

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — unless you**: 'Unless' means 'if not'. 'You cannot join unless you pay' means the same as 'you cannot join if you do not pay'. _Examples:_ You will not get the offer unless you apply today. / The deal is not valid unless you sign the contract.
- **p2 — as long as**: 'As long as' means 'if' or 'only if'. Use 'as long as' to show that something is possible or allowed under a certain condition. _Examples:_ You can get a discount as long as you show your student card. / You may use the service as long as you follow the rules.
- **p3 — provided that you**: 'Provided that' means 'if' or 'only if', like 'as long as'. Use 'provided that' in more formal situations to state a condition. _Examples:_ We will accept your application provided that you send it before Friday. / The offer is available provided that you are over 18.

**Practice block (12 items)**

1. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — A pass is required. You can't enter the building ___ you have one. → **✔ unless** · as long as · provided that
2. `p1` _Read the situation, choose_ — Your friend will come to the party, but not if she is working. What does she say? → **✔ I'll come unless I'm working.** · I'll come unless I'm not working. · I'll come as long as I'm working.
3. `p1` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "I won't go unless you come with me." → **✔ I won't go unless you come with me.** · I won't go if you come with me. · I'll go as long as you come with me.
4. `p2` _Fill the gap_ — You can borrow my bike ___ you bring it back tonight. → **✔ as long as** · as long · as long that
5. `p2` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: Can I use your laptop? / YOU: ___ → **✔ Sure, as long as you're careful with it.** · Sure, as long you're careful with it. · Sure, as long as you careful with it.
6. `p2` _Listen and arrange_ — 🔊 **You can stay as long as you are quiet.** (extra tiles: will)
7. `p3` _Fill the gap_ — The refund is possible ___ that you keep the receipt. → **✔ provided** · provide · provides

9. `p3` _Read the situation, choose_ — You write a formal email to a customer. The discount is only for people who order before Friday. What do you write? → **✔ You will get a discount provided that you order before Friday.** · You will get a discount provided that you will order before Friday. · You will get a discount unless you order before Friday.

## 21. In Case It Rains  `b1-in-case-it-rains`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** in case for precautions, and how it differs from if · **Role play targets:** in case it / just in case / take it with you

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — in case + present tense**: Use 'in case' to show you do something as a precaution, to prepare for a possible situation. You do the main action first (for example, you take an umbrella), before you know if the situation will happen. 'Just in case' can also come alone at the end: 'Take some cash, just in case.' _Examples:_ Take an umbrella in case it rains. / I will bring a jacket in case it gets cold. / Take it with you in case you need it.
- **p2 — if + present tense**: Use 'if' to talk about what you will do when or after a situation happens. The action with 'if' happens after or only when the situation happens. _Examples:_ If it rains, I will take an umbrella. / If it gets cold, I will put on my jacket.

## 22. I Wish Things Were Different  `b1-i-wish-things-were-different`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** wish and if only for regrets about the present · **Role play targets:** I wish I / if only / I wish it were

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I wish I + past simple**: Use 'I wish I' plus the past simple to talk about something you want to be different now. This shows regret or unhappiness about your present situation. For ability, use 'could' (not 'can') after 'I wish': 'I wish I could sing.' _Examples:_ I wish I had more free time. / I wish I lived closer to my friends.
- **p2 — if only + past simple**: Use 'if only' plus the past simple to show a strong wish that something in the present was different. 'If only' is often stronger than 'I wish'. _Examples:_ If only I knew the answer. / If only I had a car.
- **p3 — I wish it were**: After 'I wish it', careful and formal English prefers 'were' for unreal situations. Use 'were' in the same way after 'if only it': 'If only it were Saturday.' In everyday informal English, 'was' is also common: 'I wish it was easier.' _Examples:_ I wish it were sunny today. / I wish it were easier.

## 23. On the One Hand  `b1-on-the-one-hand`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** weighing two sides of a question out loud · **Role play targets:** on the one hand / then again / all things considered

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — on the one hand**: Use 'on the one hand' to introduce the first side or reason in a situation. This phrase signals you are starting to weigh options. _Examples:_ On the one hand, the job pays well. / On the one hand, living in the city is exciting.
- **p2 — then again**: Use 'then again' to introduce the opposite side or a different reason. This phrase shows you are considering another point. _Examples:_ Then again, the job is very stressful. / Then again, the city is very noisy.
- **p3 — all things considered**: Use 'all things considered' to give your final opinion after thinking about both sides. This phrase means you have looked at all the reasons. _Examples:_ All things considered, I think I will take the job. / All things considered, living in the city is best for me.

## 26. She Asked Where I Lived  `b1-she-asked-where-i-lived`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** reporting questions and the word order they take · **Role play targets:** she asked me / he wanted to know / they asked whether

**Teaching points**

## 35. Reporting an Incident  `b1-reporting-an-incident`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** describing what happened when you cannot say who was responsible · **Role play targets:** something was taken / it appears that / no one was hurt

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — something was taken**: Use 'something was taken' to report that an item is missing when you do not know who took it. This phrase uses the passive voice to avoid naming the person responsible. _Examples:_ Something was taken from the storage room. / We noticed that something was taken during the night.
- **p2 — it appears that**: Use 'it appears that' to describe what you think happened, based on evidence. This phrase shows you are not completely sure and avoids blaming anyone. _Examples:_ It appears that the window was broken. / It appears that someone entered through the back door.
- **p3 — no one was hurt**: Use 'no one was hurt' to say that everybody is safe after an incident. This phrase focuses on the result, not on who did something. _Examples:_ No one was hurt in the accident. / Luckily, no one was hurt when the glass broke.

## 36. How It's Made  `b1-how-its-made`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** explaining a process step by step · **Role play targets:** first it's / then it gets / after that it's

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — first it's**: Use 'first it's' to start explaining the first step of a process. This phrase helps your listener know where the process begins. _Examples:_ First it's just a piece of paper. / First it's cold water.
- **p2 — then it gets**: Use 'then it gets' to describe what happens in the next step. This phrase shows a change or development in the process. _Examples:_ Then it gets folded in half. / Then it gets heated.
