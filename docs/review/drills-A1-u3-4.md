# GlotMate — A1 units 3, 4: practice block review

## Instructions for the reviewer

You are reviewing exercise content for **GlotMate**, an app that teaches English to adult beginners through conversation with a tutor avatar. Each lesson has three parts:

1. **Lecture** — the tutor explains the teaching points (shown below as "Teaching points").
2. **Practice block** — tap-based exercises, no keyboard (shown below). Every item has exactly ONE correct answer, graded by code (no AI).
3. **Role play** — free conversation with a character; the character's first line is shown below as "Role play openings" (one per learner context: everyday, work, travel, academic, exam).

**Exercise types:** Fill the gap (choose the word) · Arrange the words (build the sentence from tiles; extra tiles are distractors) · Read the situation, choose · Tap the wrong word · Complete the chat · Match the pairs · Listen and choose (audio plays, choose what you heard) · Listen and arrange · Say this sentence (speech recognition).

**Rules the content must follow:**
- **Exactly one correct answer.** No distractor may also be acceptable in the given context. This is the most important check.
- **Level A1.** Words and grammar a A1 learner can handle; only what the lesson teaches plus very common words.
- **Coverage:** each teaching point (p1, p2…) has at least 3 items; lessons with 2+ points end with at least 2 mixed ("mix") items.
- Distractors should be plausible learner mistakes, not nonsense.
- "Arrange" items: only one correct word order. "Listen and choose": options must sound different enough to tell apart.
- "Say this sentence": no names that speech recognition could misspell.
- "Tap the wrong word": only ONE word can fix the sentence (no subject–verb agreement errors, where changing the subject would also work).
- "Arrange the words": the first tile keeps its capital and the last tile keeps the final . ? ! — so a statement cannot be rebuilt as a question.
- Anything a drill tests (and every role play target phrase) must be taught in the teaching points.
- Role play openings: very simple, end with a question, and invite the learner to use the lesson's target phrases right away. The role play is voice only: an opening may ask about the learner's OWN life or things (they can imagine them), but never about a picture or object only the character can see.

**What to return:** a list of concrete problems only (skip items that are fine). For each: lesson id, the item (quote it), the problem, and your suggested fix. Use these categories: AMBIGUOUS (more than one correct answer), WRONG (the marked answer is not correct), LEVEL (too hard / unnatural for A1), WEAK (works but could teach better), COVERAGE (a teaching point is under-practised), OPENING (role play opening issue). End with any general suggestions.

## 13. A Cat or an Apple?  `a1-a-cat-or-an-apple`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** the indefinite articles a and an · **Role play targets:** it's a book / it's an apple / it's an old

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — a / an**: Use 'a' before a word that starts with a consonant sound: a book, a car. Use 'an' before a word that starts with a vowel sound: an apple, an email. Use a or an only for one single thing you can count. _Examples:_ It's a book. / It's an apple. / I have a car.
- **p2 — It's a...**: Answer the question 'What's this?' with 'It's a' or 'It's an' plus the word. Do not leave out a or an when you name one thing. _Examples:_ What's this? It's a key. / It's an old phone.

## 14. One Book, Two Books  `a1-one-book-two-books`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** regular plurals and the most common irregular ones · **Role play targets:** two books / three children / some people

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — -s and -es**: Add -s to most words for more than one: book, books. Add -es after s, sh, ch and x: box, boxes; watch, watches. A consonant plus y becomes -ies: city, cities. _Examples:_ I have two books. / There are three boxes here. / We visited two cities.
- **p2 — children, men, women**: Some words change completely: man becomes men, woman becomes women, child becomes children. A few words do not change at all: one fish, two fish. One person, two people. 'Some people' means a few people. _Examples:_ Three children are waiting outside. / Two men and one woman came in. / There are some people outside.

5. `p1` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "I need three boxes." → **✔ I need three boxes.** · I need three box. · I need two boxes.

## 15. This, That, These, Those  `a1-this-that-these-those`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** demonstratives for things near and far · **Role play targets:** this is / that one / these are mine

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — this / these**: Use 'this' for one thing near you and 'these' for more than one near you. 'Mine' means 'my one' or 'my things': these are mine. _Examples:_ This is my seat. / These are my keys. / These bags are mine.
- **p2 — that / those**: Use 'that' for one thing far from you and 'those' for more than one far away. _Examples:_ That is your bag over there. / Those flowers are beautiful.
- **p3 — this one / that one**: Say 'this one' or 'that one' when you choose between things, so you do not repeat the word. _Examples:_ I like that one. / I'll take this one, please.

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** present simple affirmative with I, you, we and they · **Role play targets:** I wake up / we start at / they finish late

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I wake up / we start**: For things you do every day, use the plain verb after I, you, we and they. Do not add -s after these words: 'I work', not 'I works'. - **p2 — every day / on Mondays**: Add 'every day', 'every morning' or 'on Mondays' to say how often something happens. These words usually come at the end of the sentence. _Examples:_ I go to the gym every morning. / We have a meeting on Mondays.
