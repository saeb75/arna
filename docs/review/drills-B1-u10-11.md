## 55. I Enjoy Reading, I Want to Read  `b1-i-enjoy-reading-i-want-to-read`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** which common words are followed by -ing and which by to · **Role play targets:** I enjoy / I want to / I'd rather

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — 'enjoy' + verb-ing**: Use the -ing form after 'enjoy'. Do not use 'to' + verb after 'enjoy': say 'I enjoy reading', not 'I enjoy to read'. _Examples:_ I enjoy reading books. / She enjoys playing tennis.
- **p2 — 'want to' + base verb**: Use 'to' + base verb after 'want'. Do not use the -ing form after 'want': say 'I want to travel', not 'I want travelling'. _Examples:_ I want to travel this year. / They want to learn English.
- **p3 — 'would rather' + base verb**: Use the base verb (no 'to', no -ing) after 'would rather'. In speech, 'I would rather' is usually shortened to 'I'd rather'. _Examples:_ I would rather stay home tonight. / I'd rather walk to work.

## 56. I Stopped Smoking, I Stopped to Smoke  `b1-i-stopped-smoking-i-stopped-to-smoke`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** words whose meaning changes depending on -ing or to · **Role play targets:** I stopped doing / I remember meeting / I tried calling

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — stop doing vs stop to do**: 'Stop doing' means you end an action or a habit. 'Stop to do' means you stop what you are doing so that you can do something else. _Examples:_ I stopped smoking. / I stopped doing yoga last year. / I stopped to smoke.
- **p2 — remember doing vs remember to do**: Remember doing means you recall a past event. Remember to do means you do not forget to do something. _Examples:_ I remember meeting her last year. / I remembered to call her.
- **p3 — try doing vs try to do**: 'Try doing' usually means you do something as a test, to see if it solves a problem. 'Try to do' usually means you make an effort to do something difficult, often without success. The two forms can overlap; the context shows which meaning you want. _Examples:_ I tried calling her, but she did not answer. / I tried to call her, but I could not find my phone.

## 57. Look Up, Look After  `b1-look-up-look-after`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** common two-word expressions and where the object goes · **Role play targets:** look it up / look after / pick them up

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — look it up**: Some two-word expressions use a verb and a short word (particle), like look up. When the object is a pronoun (like it), put it between the verb and the particle: look it up. When the object is a noun, it can go after the expression or in the middle: look up the word / look the word up. _Examples:_ If you do not know the word, look it up. / Look up the address online.
- **p2 — look after**: Some two-word expressions do not separate; the object always goes after the particle. You cannot put the object between look and after. _Examples:_ Can you look after the kids? / She looks after her dog every day.
- **p3 — pick them up**: For expressions like pick up, put pronoun objects (like them) between the verb and the particle: pick them up. With noun objects, both orders are possible: pick up the toys / pick the toys up. Never say: pick up them. _Examples:_ Please pick them up from school. / Pick up your shoes before dinner.

**Practice block (12 items)**

1. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — I don't know this word. I'll look ___. → **✔ it up** · up it · it
2. `p1` _Arrange the words_ — **Look it up in the dictionary.** (extra tiles: looks)
3. `p1` _Read the situation, choose_ — Your friend asks what a word means. You don't know, but you have a dictionary app. What do you say? → **✔ Let's look it up.** · Let's look up it. · Let's look it.
4. `p2` _Read the situation, choose_ — You are going on holiday. You want your neighbour to water your plants and take care of them. What do you ask? → **✔ Can you look after my plants?** · Can you look my plants after? · Can you look after of my plants?
5. `p2` _Arrange the words_ — **My grandmother looks after the baby.** (extra tiles: look)
6. `p2` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "She looks after her grandmother." → **✔ She looks after her grandmother.** · She looks like her grandmother. · She lives with her grandmother.
7. `p2` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **Can you look after the kids tonight?**

## 58. Saying It More Precisely  `b1-saying-it-more-precisely`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** choosing a more exact word than the first one that comes to mind · **Role play targets:** more like / to be precise / what I mean is

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — more like**: Use 'more like' to suggest a word or description that is closer to what you mean. It helps correct or adjust your first word choice. _Examples:_ It is not broken, more like stuck. / The noise is not loud, more like annoying.
- **p2 — to be precise**: Use 'to be precise' to give a more exact detail or description. It shows you want to be clear and accurate. _Examples:_ The printer is not working. To be precise, it keeps jamming the paper. / There is a problem with the door. To be precise, it will not close fully.
- **p3 — what I mean is**: Use 'what I mean is' to explain your idea in a clearer way. It helps restate or clarify your point. _Examples:_ The screen is strange. What I mean is, it keeps flickering. / It is not just slow. What I mean is, the computer freezes a lot.

## 59. It's Worth Trying  `b1-its-worth-trying`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** fixed expressions that take an -ing word after them · **Role play targets:** it's worth / there's no point / I can't help

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — it's worth + -ing**: Use 'it's worth' followed by a verb ending in -ing to say something is a good idea or useful. Do not use the to-infinitive after 'it's worth'. _Examples:_ It's worth trying this new restaurant. / It's worth learning a new skill.
- **p2 — there's no point + -ing**: Use 'there's no point' followed by a verb ending in -ing to say something is useless or not necessary. You can also say 'there's no point in' + -ing: there's no point in waiting. Do not use the to-infinitive after 'there's no point'. _Examples:_ There's no point arguing about it. / There's no point waiting any longer.
- **p3 — I can't help + -ing**: Use 'I can't help' followed by a verb ending in -ing to say you cannot stop yourself from doing something. Here 'help' does not mean 'give help', so do not use the to-infinitive after it. _Examples:_ I can't help laughing at his jokes. / I can't help thinking about the problem.

## 60. Although, However, Despite  `b1-although-however-despite`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** showing contrast between two ideas in one stretch of speech · **Role play targets:** although it was / even though / despite that

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — although it was**: Use 'although' to join two facts that contrast. The 'although' part can come first or second: 'Although it was cold, we went out.' / 'We went out although it was cold.' When the 'although' part comes first, put a comma after it. After 'although it was', you can use an adjective or a noun (although it was cold / a holiday), or a verb with -ing for an action in progress (although it was raining). _Examples:_ Although it was raining, we went for a walk. / We enjoyed the picnic although it was cold.
- **p2 — even though**: 'Even though' works like 'although', but the contrast is stronger. Put 'even though' before the fact; the other part of the sentence gives the surprising result. Like 'although', it can come first or second in the sentence. _Examples:_ Even though I was tired, I finished my homework. / She smiled even though she felt sad.
- **p3 — despite that**: 'Despite that' refers back to the previous statement and introduces a contrasting result. The first sentence gives a fact; 'despite that' starts the second sentence. Follow 'despite that' with the surprising action or result. 'However' can start the second sentence in the same way: It was late. However, they kept working. _Examples:_ The exam was very difficult. Despite that, he passed. / It was late. Despite that, they kept working.

**Practice block (12 items)**

1. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — ___ it was cold, we had a picnic. → **✔ Although** · Despite · In spite

4. `p1` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **Although it was late, we kept talking.**
5. `p2` _Fill the gap_ — She went to work ___ she had a fever. → **✔ even though** · despite · even
6. `p2` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: Did you enjoy the party? / YOU: ___ → **✔ Yes, even though I didn't know many people.** · Yes, even I didn't know many people. · Yes, despite I didn't know many people.

## 61. Explaining Yourself Clearly  `b1-explaining-yourself-clearly`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** explaining something complicated in simple words · **Role play targets:** let me put it / in other words / does that make sense

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — let me put it**: Use 'let me put it' to start explaining something in a different or simpler way. This phrase shows you want to help the listener understand. _Examples:_ Let me put it another way: it is like a big team project. / Let me put it simply: you just need to follow these steps.
- **p2 — in other words**: Use 'in other words' to say the same thing in a simpler or clearer way. It helps the listener see your point from a new angle. _Examples:_ In other words, you just have to wait your turn. / It is very expensive. In other words, not many people can buy it.
- **p3 — does that make sense**: Use 'does that make sense' to check if the listener understands your explanation. It invites questions if something is not clear. _Examples:_ You add water, then mix it. Does that make sense? / It works like this: you press the button and it starts. Does that make sense?

3. `p1` _Listen and arrange_ — 🔊 **Let me put it in a different way.** (extra tiles: puts)
4. `p2` _Fill the gap_ — The flight is full. In other ___, there are no seats left. → **✔ words** · word · ways
