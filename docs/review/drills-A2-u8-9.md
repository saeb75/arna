## 53. Would You Mind?  `a2-would-you-mind`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** making polite requests and answering them · **Role play targets:** would you mind / not at all / of course not

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — would you mind + -ing**: 'Would you mind' is followed by a verb with -ing, not by 'to'. It is one of the most polite ways to ask a stranger for something. _Examples:_ Would you mind moving your bag? / Would you mind waiting a moment?
- **p2 — not at all**: The question asks whether something bothers you, so 'no' means yes. 'Not at all' and 'of course not' both mean you agree happily. _Examples:_ Not at all, go ahead. / Of course not, take your time.
- **p3 — sorry, I'm afraid**: To refuse, start with 'sorry' and give a short reason. A bare 'no' sounds abrupt in this situation. _Examples:_ Sorry, I'm afraid I need it. / I'm afraid I can't right now.

## 54. I'm Sorry, I Can't  `a2-im-sorry-i-cant`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** saying no politely and apologising · **Role play targets:** I'm afraid I can't / sorry about that / maybe next time

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I'm afraid I can't**: 'I'm afraid' softens bad news and is not about fear. It usually comes before the refusal, not after it. _Examples:_ I'm afraid I can't make it on Friday. / I'm afraid that won't work for me.
- **p2 — sorry about that**: A short reason makes a refusal easier to accept. The reason does not need to be detailed or private. _Examples:_ I have something on that evening, sorry about that. / Sorry about that, it's a busy week.
- **p3 — maybe next time**: 'Maybe next time' keeps the relationship open. Offering another day shows the refusal is about timing, not the person. _Examples:_ Maybe next time. / Could we do it the following week instead?

## 55. A Small Favour  `a2-a-small-favour`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** asking neighbours and colleagues for favours politely · **Role play targets:** would you mind / could you please / no problem

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — would you mind**: With people you know, 'would you mind' still works and stays polite. Adding the reason first makes the favour easier to accept. _Examples:_ I'll be away next week. ## 57. Something Went Wrong  `a2-something-went-wrong`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** complaining politely and returning something · **Role play targets:** there's a problem / it doesn't work / could I get a refund

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — there's a problem**: Open with the problem, calmly and without blaming the person in front of you. 'There's a problem with ...' is neutral and easy to answer. _Examples:_ There's a problem with this kettle. / There's a problem with my order.
- **p2 — it doesn't work**: Say exactly what is wrong: it doesn't work, it's broken, a part is missing. A precise fault gets a faster answer than a general complaint. _Examples:_ It doesn't work at all. / It stopped working after two days.
- **p3 — could I get a refund**: Say what you want: a refund, a replacement or a repair. 'Could I' keeps the request polite even when you are annoyed. _Examples:_ Could I get a refund, please? / Could I exchange it for a new one?

## 60. The Man Who Helped Me  `a2-the-man-who-helped-me`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** joining sentences with who, which and that · **Role play targets:** someone who / something that / people who live

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — someone who**: 'Who' introduces defining information about a person. In informal defining clauses, 'that' can often replace 'who'. _Examples:_ The man who helped me was very kind. / I need someone who speaks Spanish.
- **p2 — something that**: 'Which' and 'that' introduce defining information about a thing. 'That' is very common in everyday defining clauses. _Examples:_ It's a thing that opens bottles. / The bag which I lost was blue.
- **p3 — people who live**: The describing part comes straight after the noun it describes. Do not repeat the noun with a pronoun inside the clause. _Examples:_ People who live here know the shortcuts. / The train that leaves at six is faster.

**Practice block (12 items)**

1. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — I need someone ___ speaks Spanish. → **✔ who** · which · what
2. `p1` _Arrange the words_ — **The man who helped me was very kind.** (extra tiles: which)

## 62. The Other One  `a2-the-other-one`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** other, another and the others · **Role play targets:** I prefer the other / another one / what about the others

**Teaching points**

## 63. I Used to Play Football  `a2-i-used-to-play-football`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** 'used to' for past habits that have stopped · **Role play targets:** I used to / we used to / didn't use to

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I used to**: 'Used to' plus the base verb describes a repeated past action or a past situation that is no longer true. - **p2 — didn't use to**: In questions and negatives the -d disappears: did you use to, didn't use to. This is because 'did' already carries the past. _Examples:_ Did you use to play an instrument? / I didn't use to like coffee.

## 64. What Do You Think?  `a2-what-do-you-think`

**Teaching points**
