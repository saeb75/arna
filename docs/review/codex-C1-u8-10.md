| 46 | “With respect, I'd argue the opposite, and I see it differently.” | WEAK | The two clauses repeat the same disagreement and sound mechanically stacked. Use a substantive second clause, e.g. “With respect, I'd argue remote work can improve results. I see the evidence differently.” |
| 46 | Work opening: “I have decided we cancel the training week…” | OPENING | “Decided we cancel” is unidiomatic here. Change it to “I’ve decided that we should cancel the training week…” or “I’ve decided to cancel…” |
| 47 | “Naming the person also stops the loudest voice filling the gap.” | WEAK | This overstates the effect: naming someone cannot guarantee that others will not interrupt. Use “helps stop” or “makes it less likely that…” |
| 47 | “We're running short, so coming back to the budget.” | WEAK | The clause after “so” is an awkward fragment. Use “We’re running short of time. Coming back to the budget, where are we?” |
| 47 | Exam opening: “I have a great deal more to say on this. May I continue?” | OPENING | It makes the candidate manage or silence the examiner, which does not fit a normal exam relationship. Explicitly frame it as a simulation: “Imagine I’m a participant who is taking too long. How would you bring the discussion back?” |
| 48 | Listen and arrange: “That's not something we can change.” | AMBIGUOUS | The same tiles also form valid English: “That’s something we can not change.” It has a different possible meaning but is still grammatical. Change this to Listen and choose, or use a sentence without movable “not,” such as “We can’t change that point.” |
| 49 | Academic opening: “You asked me to come in. Is there news about my exchange place?” | OPENING | A learner would not normally control a tutor’s exchange-place decision. Make the character an applicant or student rather than the learner’s tutor. |
| 49 | Exam opening: “I have been waiting to hear. What is the decision?” | OPENING | The learner is given neither a decision nor a clear simulated role, yet must deliver bad news to the examiner. Supply both: “Imagine you must reject my application. What would you say?” |
| 50 | “What I've observed is that the last three deadlines slipped.” | WRONG | If the colleague failed to meet them, the deadlines were “missed”; “deadlines slipped” normally suggests that the dates themselves moved. Use “What I’ve observed is that the last three deadlines were missed.” |
| 50 | Academic opening: “You wanted to see me about my essays. What's this about?” | OPENING | It reverses the normal tutor–student role and supplies no plausible basis for the learner to assess the tutor’s essays. Use a peer or student character and give an observable issue, such as three late drafts. |
| 50 | Exam opening: “You wanted to talk about my work. What is this about?” | OPENING | The candidate is made responsible for evaluating the examiner, with no observed behaviour provided. Frame it explicitly as a role-play and give a fact: “In this role-play, my last three reports were late. What did you want to discuss?” |
| 52 | “What we're doing now is moving customers.” | WEAK | “Moving customers” is incomplete and can suggest physically relocating people. Preserve the supplied fact: “What we’re doing now is moving customers to a backup system.” |
| 52 | Exam opening: “Something has gone wrong and nobody will tell me what. Well?” | OPENING | The learner receives no confirmed facts, current action or update time, so the target phrases cannot be used meaningfully without inventing the entire crisis. Give a minimal scenario, such as two systems being down while engineers check them. |
| 53 | Exam opening: “Could you still get a place on the course this year?” | OPENING | Nothing establishes whether success is unlikely or the opportunity has gone permanently, so neither “a long shot” nor “that ship has sailed” is justified. Add a fact: “Applications closed yesterday, but one place may reopen. Is there any chance?” |
| 54 | “There was heavy rain all day.” / “There was strong rain all day.” | AMBIGUOUS | “Strong rain” occurs in regional English and in some descriptive usage. It is unsafe as an absolutely incorrect, code-graded distractor. Ask which is the *usual standard British/American collocation*, or replace the item with one that does not reject a regional variant. |
| 54 | Academic opening: “Why is ‘strong rain’ wrong when nothing in the grammar forbids it?” | OPENING | This contradicts the teaching point, which calls it grammatical but unidiomatic. Change “wrong” to “unusual in standard English.” |
| 57 | “Reading the mood, I'll stop there.” | LEVEL | Clause-initial “Reading the mood” sounds contrived as a spoken discourse marker. The conventional expression is “reading the room,” which also matches the lesson title. Change the p3 target and its exercises to “Reading the room…” or use “Judging by the mood…” |
| 57 | Exam opening: “Tell me, what do you actually earn in your job?” | OPENING | “Maybe not here” is an illogical response in a private exam: there is no plausible later or more suitable setting. Ask about a sensitive subject in a clearly public or mixed-company scenario instead. |
| 59 | “The listener will wait through a digression they know is temporary.” | WEAK | This is an unjustified guarantee. Use “is more likely to follow” or “may be more willing to wait through…” |
| 60 | “Naming the order also stops you rearranging it halfway through.” | WEAK | Signposting does not prevent rearrangement. It merely commits the speaker publicly and makes changes more noticeable. Rewrite accordingly. |
| 61 | “I'd distinguish between cause or blame.” | AMBIGUOUS | Although edited standard English strongly prefers “between X and Y,” “between X or Y” is common enough in informal speech to be unsafe as a code-graded distractor. Use a fluent but meaningfully wrong option, e.g. “I’d treat cause and blame as the same thing.” |
| 61 | “Is it better to be respected or liked?” → “I'd distinguish between two kinds of respect.” | WEAK | The response does not address the stated contrast between respect and liking. Use “I’d distinguish between being respected and being liked.” |
| 63 | “From what little I know, the batteries are the problem.” | WEAK | The hedge is modest, but “are the problem” remains categorical and overgeneralised. Use “battery cost may be one of the main problems.” |
| 65 | Listen and arrange: “The service falls short of the price.” | WEAK | Service does not idiomatically “fall short of” a price; it falls short of expectations or of what the price promises. Use “The service falls short of what the price suggests.” |
| 65 | “The view lives up to its reputation, but the food falls short of it.” | WRONG | The nearest antecedent of “it” is the view’s reputation, so the sentence compares the food with the view’s reputation. Use “The view lives up to its reputation, but the food falls short of the restaurant’s reputation.” |
| 65 | Everyday opening: “Everyone says it is incredible. Is it, though?” | OPENING | “It” has no shared referent in this voice-only opening. Name the thing: “Everyone says the new café is incredible. Is it, though?” |
| 65 | Travel opening: “Two hours in line for the famous tower. Was it worth the queue?” | OPENING | This invites “worth the queue/wait,” not the taught “worth the hype,” “lives up to,” or “falls short of.” Use “Was the tower as good as everyone said?” |
| 65 | Academic opening: “Everyone calls this novel a masterpiece. Do you agree?” | OPENING | “This novel” refers to an unidentified item that only the character appears to know. Name a novel or say “a novel you recently read.” |

General suggestions:

- Most distractors test elementary morphology rather than C1 judgement: forms such as “we knows,” “readed,” “straightly,” and “I'd avoiding” are too easy. Prefer grammatical, fluent distractors that are wrong because of meaning, register, collocation or conversational purpose.
- Automatically permutation-test every Arrange item, including movement of negatives and auxiliaries. Audio does not cure a tile set that can form another valid English sentence under the stated rule.
- Give role-play openings enough shared facts for the learner to use the target immediately. Exam simulations should explicitly assign the imagined role, and voice-only openings should name any referenced book, place, product or event.
