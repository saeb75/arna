## 22. Framing the Question  `c2-framing-the-question`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** defining the terms before the argument starts · **Role play targets:** before we start let's / what exactly do we mean / that's the real question

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — before we start let's**: 'Before we start, let's agree what X means' puts the definition ahead of the dispute; 'let's agree that X means Y' goes further and proposes one. The definition shapes what counts as evidence, so proposing a specific one is itself a move in the argument. _Examples:_ Before we start, let's agree what 'fair' means. / Before we start, let's define the scope.
- **p2 — what exactly do we mean**: 'What exactly do we mean by X?' asks for the definition rather than supplying it. Asking commits you less than proposing, and it can expose a disagreement a supplied definition would conceal. _Examples:_ What exactly do we mean by 'success'? / What exactly do we mean here?
- **p3 — that's the real question**: 'That's the real question' relocates the argument to the point you think decides it. It only lands if the relocation is genuine; used to dodge, it is heard as changing the subject. _Examples:_ That's the real question, not the cost. / That's the real question underneath it.

## 23. Attacking an Argument  `c2-attacking-an-argument`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** going after the reasoning rather than the person · **Role play targets:** that assumes / your premise is / that doesn't follow

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — that assumes**: 'That assumes X' surfaces a premise the other side did not state. It is only an attack if the assumption is genuinely doing work; naming a harmless one wastes the move. _Examples:_ That assumes demand stays flat. / That assumes everyone has a car.
- **p2 — your premise is**: 'Your premise is X, and I dispute it' attacks the starting point rather than the conclusion. Disputing a premise undermines the conclusion if that premise is necessary to it; otherwise the remaining premises may still carry it. _Examples:_ Your premise is that cost is fixed. / Your premise is one I'd dispute.
- **p3 — that doesn't follow**: 'That doesn't follow' says the conclusion is not supported even if the premises are true. It obliges you to show the gap; asserted alone it is just contradiction. _Examples:_ That doesn't follow from what you said. / Even so, that doesn't follow.

## 24. Concession as Strategy  `c2-concession-as-strategy`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** giving ground deliberately in order to gain it · **Role play targets:** I'll grant you that / you're right about / even accepting that

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I'll grant you that**: 'I'll grant you that' concedes a point openly, which makes your remaining position look examined. Concede a less central point and the move is cheap; concede the one the decision turns on and you have lost. _Examples:_ I'll grant you that the cost is high. / I'll grant you that much.
- **p2 — you're right about**: 'You're right about X' names exactly what you accept, which stops the concession spreading. An unbounded 'you're right' is often heard as agreeing with the whole case. _Examples:_ You're right about the timing. / You're right about the first part.
- **p3 — even accepting that**: 'Even accepting that, Y' shows the conclusion survives the concession. This is where the concession pays: without it you have simply agreed with them. _Examples:_ Even accepting that, the case holds. / Even accepting that, I'd still delay.

## 25. A Formal Debate  `c2-a-formal-debate`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** arguing a clear motion under debate conventions · **Role play targets:** I oppose the motion / my opponent claims / I put it to you

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I oppose the motion**: 'I oppose the motion' states your side before your reasons, as most debating formats require. In a formal debate you argue the assigned side, so the phrase reports a position, not a belief. _Examples:_ I oppose the motion before us. / I oppose the motion on two grounds.
- **p2 — my opponent claims**: 'My opponent claims X' restates their case before you answer it. A restatement they would recognise is required; a convenient one is a straw man and is scored as such. _Examples:_ My opponent claims the cost is trivial. / My opponent claims this is settled.
- **p3 — I put it to you**: In a debate, 'I put it to you that X' puts your claim to the audience or panel for their judgement. In cross-examination the same phrase is put to a witness; in a debate, aiming it at those who decide, not at the opponent, is deliberate. _Examples:_ I put it to you that the reverse is true. / I put it to you plainly.

## 26. Devil's Advocate  `c2-devils-advocate`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** arguing a case you personally do not believe · **Role play targets:** for the sake of argument / someone has to say / let me push back

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — for the sake of argument**: 'For the sake of argument' asks the listener to entertain a case for now, without committing you to it. Without the marker a strong argument is attributed to you, and denying it later looks like retreat. _Examples:_ For the sake of argument, suppose it works. / For the sake of argument only, let's suppose the figures are accurate.
- **p2 — someone has to say**: 'Someone has to say it' frames an unpopular point as necessary to raise; it does not distance you from the point. It works where the point is genuinely unsaid; where others have said it, it sounds self-flattering. _Examples:_ Someone has to say it. / Someone has to say the obvious thing.
- **p3 — let me push back**: 'Let me push back on that' signals a challenge; extra framing is needed to show it is only a test. Paired with 'for the sake of argument' it invites the other side to strengthen their case rather than defend themselves. _Examples:_ Let me push back on that. / Let me push back for a moment.

**Practice block (11 items)**

1. `p1` _Read the situation, choose_ — Your team loves a plan. You don't think it will fail, but you want to test it by arguing that it might, without people thinking that's your real view. What do you say? → **✔ For the sake of argument, suppose the plan fails.** · Honestly, I think the plan will fail. · For the sake of argue, suppose the plan fails.

4. `p2` _Read the situation, choose_ — Everyone in the room is thinking the deadline is impossible, but nobody has said it yet. You decide to raise it. 5. `p2` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: Why are you the one bringing this up? / YOU: ___ → **✔ Because someone has to say it.** · Because someone has to saying it. · Because someone have to say it.
6. `p2` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **Someone has to say the obvious thing.**
7. `p3` _Read the situation, choose_ — A colleague says the plan is ready to go. You want to challenge that before anyone decides. What do you say? → **✔ Let me push back on that.** · Let me just agree with that. · Let me back push on that.
8. `p3` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "Let me push back for a moment." → **✔ Let me push back for a moment.** · Let me think about it for a moment. · Let me get my coat first.
9. `p3` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: So we're all agreed on the budget? / YOU: ___ → **✔ Let me push back on that before we decide.** · Let me pushing back on that before we decide. · Let me pushed back on that before we decide.

## 27. The Uncomfortable Question  `c2-the-uncomfortable-question`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** answering the question you would rather avoid · **Role play targets:** that's a fair question / I won't pretend / honestly I don't know

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — that's a fair question**: 'That's a fair question' grants the question rather than resenting it. Said and then not answered, it becomes the most obvious deflection available. _Examples:_ That's a fair question. / That's a fair question and I'll answer it.
- **p2 — I won't pretend**: 'I won't pretend X' concedes the difficulty before anyone forces you to. Conceding first costs less than being caught concealing, and it buys credit for the rest of the answer. _Examples:_ I won't pretend it went well. / I won't pretend we saw it coming.
- **p3 — honestly I don't know**: 'Honestly, I don't know' is an answer, and often a better one than a manufactured explanation. Where finding the answer is your responsibility, follow it with what you will do; otherwise it can sound disengaged. _Examples:_ Honestly, I don't know yet. / Honestly, I don't know, and I'll find out.

## 42. Holding an Audience  `c2-holding-an-audience`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** sustaining a long turn that nobody wants to interrupt · **Role play targets:** bear with me / this is the part / you'll see why

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — bear with me**: 'Bear with me' asks for patience explicitly, which is easier to grant than to assume. It creates an expectation that the relevance will become clear soon. _Examples:_ Bear with me, this connects. / Bear with me for one minute.
- **p2 — this is the part**: 'This is the part that matters' tells the listener where to spend their attention. Repeated use weakens its ability to mark any one point as the central one. _Examples:_ This is the part that matters. / This is the part I keep thinking about.
- **p3 — you'll see why**: 'You'll see why in a moment' justifies a detail whose relevance is not yet clear. It is a promise about the near future, so a long delay before the payoff breaks it. _Examples:_ You'll see why in a moment. / You'll see why that matters.

**Practice block (11 items)**

1. `p1` _Read the situation, choose_ — You are about to give background that seems off-topic, but it connects soon. You want to ask your listeners for patience. What do you say? → **✔ Bear with me, this connects.** · Sorry, this is boring, I'll stop. · Bear me with, this connects.
2. `p1` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: Sorry, why are we talking about suppliers? / YOU: ___ → **✔ Bear with me for one minute.** · Bear for me with one minute. · Bearing with me for one minute.
