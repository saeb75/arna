## 1. Not Only Did She Come  `c1-not-only-did-she-come`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** flipping the word order after a negative opener for emphasis · **Role play targets:** not only did / no sooner had / not once did

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — not only did**: After a fronted negative the auxiliary moves in front of the subject. If there is no auxiliary, 'do', 'does' or 'did' supplies one. _Examples:_ Not only did she come, she stayed all evening. / Not only was it late, it was the wrong address.
- **p2 — no sooner had**: 'No sooner ... than' pairs with the past perfect in the first half. 'Than' is the standard partner here, not 'when'. _Examples:_ No sooner had we sat down than the phone rang. / No sooner had it started than it stopped.
- **p3 — not once did**: After a fronted 'not once' or 'never', inversion is required; the real choice is whether to front it at all. Because it draws attention, repeated use in one stretch weakens the effect. _Examples:_ Not once did he apologise. / Never in ten years had I seen that.

**Practice block (12 items)**

1. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — Not only ___ he forget the tickets, he lost the map too. → **✔ did** · was · had
2. `p1` _Read the situation, choose_ — Your friend's party had live music, and on top of that the food was free. You want to stress both. What do you say? → **✔ Not only was there live music, the food was free too.** · Not only there was live music, the food was free too. · Not only did there was live music, the food was free too.
3. `p1` _Tap the wrong word_ — Not only did she came, she stayed all evening. → wrong word: **came,** → Not only did she come, she stayed all evening.
4. `p2` _Fill the gap_ — No sooner had we arrived ___ it started to rain. → **✔ than** · then · so
5. `p2` _Listen and arrange_ — 🔊 **No sooner had I sat down than the phone rang.**
6. `p2` _Read the situation, choose_ — You opened the window, and a moment later it began to rain heavily. How do you tell it with emphasis? → **✔ No sooner had I opened the window than it began to rain.** · No sooner I had opened the window than it began to rain. · No sooner did I had opened the window than it began to rain.
7. `p3` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: Did he ever say sorry for the mess? / YOU: ___ → **✔ No. Not once did he say sorry.** · No. Not once he said sorry. · No. Not once did he said sorry.
8. `p3` _Fill the gap_ — Not once last year ___ they ask for my opinion. → **✔ did** · do · were
9. `p3` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "Not once did anyone thank us." → **✔ Not once did anyone thank us.** · Not one person was there to thank us. · Once again, everyone thanked us.
10. `p3` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **Not once did he apologise.**
11. `mix` _Read the situation, choose_ — The train was late, and the moment you got on, it broke down. Tell it with emphasis. → **✔ Not only was the train late; no sooner had we boarded than it broke down.** · Not only the train was late; no sooner had we boarded than it broke down. · Not only was the train late; no sooner we had boarded than it broke down.
12. `mix` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "Not only did she call; not once did she complain." → **✔ Not only did she call; not once did she complain.** · Not only she called; once she complained. · She did not call, and once she complained.

## 2. Only Then Did I Realise  `c1-only-then-did-i-realise`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** flipping the order after only, not until and no sooner · **Role play targets:** only then did / only after / not until then

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — only then did**: 'Only then' or 'only later' at the front inverts the subject and the auxiliary. If there is no auxiliary, 'did' supplies one: 'Only later did we find out'. _Examples:_ Only then did I realise what had happened. / Only later did we find out.
- **p2 — only after**: With 'only after' or 'only when' plus a clause, the inversion falls on the main clause, not on the clause after 'only'. Say 'only after she left did I understand', not 'did she leave I understood'. _Examples:_ Only after she left did I understand. / Only when it was over did anyone speak.
- **p3 — not until then**: 'Not until' works the same way: the inversion falls on the main clause. The effect is to make the listener wait for the point, which is the purpose. _Examples:_ Not until then did I see the pattern. / Not until the letter arrived did we believe it.

**Practice block (11 items)**

1. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — Only then ___ we understand why she had left. → **✔ did** · were · have
2. `p1` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: When did you realise the shop was closed? / YOU: ___ → **✔ When I saw the sign. Only then did I realise.** · When I saw the sign. Only then I did realise. · When I saw the sign. Only then did I realised.
3. `p1` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "Only then did I see the problem." → **✔ Only then did I see the problem.** · Even then I didn't see the problem. · Only the end showed me the problem.
4. `p2` _Read the situation, choose_ — You only understood her advice once she had gone. Which sentence is correct? → **✔ Only after she had gone did I see her point.** · Only after did she go I saw her point. · Only after she had gone I did see her point.
5. `p2` _Fill the gap_ — Only after the meeting ended ___ anyone mention the budget. → **✔ did** · had · was
6. `p2` _Arrange the words_ — **Only after the call did I relax.** (extra tiles: relaxed)
7. `p3` _Fill the gap_ — Not until the results came out ___ we celebrate. → **✔ did** · were · have

9. `p3` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **Not until then did I see it.**

## 3. It Is Precisely This That  `c1-it-is-precisely-this-that`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** advanced front-loading to pinpoint one element · **Role play targets:** it is precisely / what matters here is / this is exactly what

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — it is precisely**: 'Precisely' and 'exactly' sharpen a cleft so the focus cannot be missed. The neutral, most common position is between 'it is' and the highlighted element. _Examples:_ It is precisely this that worries me. / It was exactly then that things changed.
- **p2 — what matters here is**: A what-clause delays the key element to the end of the sentence. It is common in argument because the listener hears the claim last. _Examples:_ What matters here is the timing. / What concerns me is the precedent.
- **p3 — this is exactly what**: 'This is exactly what I meant' points back at something already said. Pointing back is what distinguishes it from the forward-pointing cleft. _Examples:_ This is exactly what I was afraid of. / This is exactly what we discussed.

**Practice block (11 items)**

1. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — It is ___ this point that I disagree with. → **✔ precisely** · precise · precision
2. `p1` _Read the situation, choose_ — A colleague thinks you object to the cost, but your real worry is the deadline. What do you say? → **✔ It is precisely the deadline that worries me.** · It is precisely the deadline what worries me. · It is precise the deadline that worries me.
3. `p1` _Tap the wrong word_ — It is precise this that worries me. → wrong word: **precise** → It is precisely this that worries me.
4. `p2` _Fill the gap_ — What matters here ___ the timing, not the cost. → **✔ is** · are · be
5. `p2` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "What matters here is trust." → **✔ What matters here is trust.** · Trust matters here, not what we say. · What does it matter here?
6. `p2` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: So you think the price is the real problem? / YOU: ___ → **✔ Not really. What matters here is the timing.** · Not really. What matters here are the timing. · Not really. What matter here is the timing.
7. `p3` _Fill the gap_ — This is ___ what I was worried about. → **✔ exactly** · exact · exactness
8. `p3` _Read the situation, choose_ — Your friend has just described the very problem you warned about last week. What do you say? → **✔ This is exactly what I warned you about.** · This is exactly that I warned you about. · This is exactly which I warned you about.

11. `mix` _Read the situation, choose_ — You want to point to one thing only: the tone, not the words. What do you say? → **✔ What matters here is the tone, not the words.** · This is exactly what the tone, not the words. · It is precisely the tone what matters, not the words.

## 4. What Struck Me Most  `c1-what-struck-me-most`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** foregrounding your own reaction to something · **Role play targets:** what struck me / what stood out / what I noticed was

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — what struck me**: 'What struck me was...' reports an impression rather than a verdict. It invites agreement more easily than a flat judgement does. _Examples:_ What struck me was how quiet it was. / What struck me most was the scale.
- **p2 — what stood out**: 'What stood out' singles out the most noticeable detail without judging it as good or bad. It works well when you do not want to summarise everything. _Examples:_ What stood out was the tone. / What stood out for me was the ending.
- **p3 — what I noticed was**: 'What I noticed was' is the most neutral of the three. It reports observation without claiming the observation matters. _Examples:_ What I noticed was how few people spoke. / What I noticed was the change in pace.

## 5. Driving a Point Home  `c1-driving-a-point-home`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** arguing with deliberate emphasis so the key point cannot be missed · **Role play targets:** let me be clear / make no mistake / I cannot stress enough

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — let me be clear**: 'Let me be clear' announces that what follows is the position, not a nuance. Repeated use can make it sound defensive and reduce its force. _Examples:_ Let me be clear about what I am not saying. / Let me be clear: this is not about money.
- **p2 — make no mistake**: 'Make no mistake' warns against a misreading you expect. It is strong, so it fits a genuine risk of being misunderstood. _Examples:_ Make no mistake, this will cost us. / Make no mistake about the timescale.
- **p3 — I cannot stress enough**: 'I cannot stress enough' marks a point as the one to remember. Because it claims maximum weight, a second use in the same stretch undercuts the first. _Examples:_ I cannot stress enough how urgent this is. / I cannot stress this enough.

**Practice block (11 items)**

## 6. Understated but Firm  `c1-understated-but-firm`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** making a strong point quietly rather than loudly · **Role play targets:** I'd simply note / it's worth remembering / I'll leave it there

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I'd simply note**: 'I'd simply note' presents a strong point as a small observation. The understatement is what gives it weight in a heated exchange. _Examples:_ I'd simply note that the figures say otherwise. / I'd simply note the timing.
- **p2 — it's worth remembering**: 'It's worth remembering' reintroduces an inconvenient fact without accusation. It reminds without openly accusing anyone, though a sharp tone can still make it pointed. _Examples:_ It's worth remembering who raised it first. / It's worth remembering what was agreed.
- **p3 — I'll leave it there**: 'I'll leave it there' closes your contribution without conceding. Stopping while ahead is itself a rhetorical move. _Examples:_ I'll leave it there. / I'll leave it there for now.

## 7. Were I to Suggest  `c1-were-i-to-suggest`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** hypothetical sentences built without if · **Role play targets:** were I to / were it not / should that happen

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — were I to**: 'Were I to suggest' replaces 'if I suggested' and sounds markedly more formal. The pattern is: were + subject + to + base verb. _Examples:_ Were I to suggest an alternative, would you consider it? / Were we to agree, what then?
- **p2 — were it not**: 'Were it not for' names the one thing preventing an outcome. It replaces 'if it were not for' with no change of meaning; 'were it not so late' replaces 'if it were not so late'. _Examples:_ Were it not for the cost, I would agree. / Were it not so late, we could start now.
- **p3 — should that happen**: 'Should' at the front makes a future possibility more remote and more formal. 'Should you need anything' replaces 'if you need anything'. _Examples:_ Should that happen, we will need a plan. / Should you need anything, do ask.

## 23. Institutional Language  `c1-institutional-language`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** the standard vocabulary of official communication · **Role play targets:** with regard to / in accordance with / pursuant to

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — with regard to**: 'With regard to' introduces the subject and replaces 'about'. 'With regards to' is common but often marked as an error in formal writing; use 'with regard to'. _Examples:_ With regard to your enquiry, we can confirm the following. / With regard to the deadline, an extension applies.
- **p2 — in accordance with**: 'In accordance with' cites the rule an action follows. It is followed by a noun: a policy, a regulation, an agreement. _Examples:_ In accordance with the policy, we have refunded the fee. / In accordance with your request, the payment has been made.
- **p3 — pursuant to**: 'Pursuant to' is the most formal of the three and is largely legal. Outside legal or regulatory text it can obstruct rather than help. _Examples:_ Pursuant to section four, notice is required. / Pursuant to the agreement, notice must be given in writing.

## 24. A Formal Complaint  `c1-a-formal-complaint`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** raising a serious issue in fully formal English · **Role play targets:** I wish to raise / I would draw attention / I trust this will

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I wish to raise**: 'I wish to raise' opens a formal complaint without emotion. Keeping emotion out keeps the focus on the evidence and the remedy you want. _Examples:_ I wish to raise a serious concern. / I wish to raise the matter formally.
- **p2 — I would draw attention**: 'I would draw your attention to' points at evidence without accusing. It lets the facts carry the accusation instead of you. _Examples:_ I would draw your attention to the attached record. / I would draw attention to the dates.
- **p3 — I trust this will**: 'I trust this will be resolved' states an expectation rather than a threat. It states a firm expectation without sounding openly threatening. _Examples:_ I trust this will be addressed promptly. / I trust this will not recur.

## 25. Presenting to a Board  `c1-presenting-to-a-board`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** delivering a short, structured formal presentation · **Role play targets:** I'd like to begin / turning to the second / to conclude

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I'd like to begin**: 'I'd like to begin by...' tells the audience what the first section covers. Signposting matters most with an audience that will not interrupt. _Examples:_ I'd like to begin by setting out the problem. / I'd like to begin with the numbers.
- **p2 — turning to the second**: 'Turning to' marks a section boundary out loud. Explicit boundaries help an audience that will not interrupt to follow the structure. _Examples:_ Turning to the second point, I'd like to address costs. / Turning now to the risks.
- **p3 — to conclude**: In a board presentation, 'to conclude' should usually lead to the decision you want, not only a summary. A presentation without an ask leaves the audience unsure what to do. _Examples:_ To conclude, I would ask the board to approve the pilot. / To conclude, we recommend option two.
