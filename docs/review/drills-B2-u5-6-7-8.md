## 29. Having Finished, She Left  `b2-having-finished-she-left`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** opening a sentence with having plus a finished action · **Role play targets:** having finished / having said that / having done that

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — having finished**: 'Having' plus the past participle shows the first action was complete before the second. The understood subject of the participle must be the subject of the main clause, or it dangles. _Examples:_ Having finished the report, she left. / Having checked everything, we set off.
- **p2 — having said that**: 'Having said that' is a fixed phrase meaning 'despite what I just said'. It works as a link between two contrasting statements, not as a time marker. _Examples:_ Having said that, I would still recommend it. / Having said that, the price is high.
- **p3 — having done that**: The structure belongs to written and careful spoken English rather than casual chat. In everyday speech 'after she finished' says the same thing more simply. _Examples:_ Having done that, he moved on to the next task. / After she finished, she left.

## 30. Built in 1920, It Still Stands  `b2-built-in-1920-it-still-stands`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** opening a sentence with a done-form to add background · **Role play targets:** built in / founded in / written by hand

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — built in**: A past participle at the start carries a passive meaning: built means 'which was built'. The thing described must be the subject of the main clause. _Examples:_ Built in 1920, the bridge still stands. / Written in a week, the report was rushed.

- **p3 — written by hand**: The participle can carry its own extra information: written by hand, painted in oils. A comma separates this opening from the main clause. _Examples:_ Written entirely by hand, the letter took hours to complete. / Painted in oils, it has lasted centuries.

## 31. The Man Standing Over There  `b2-the-man-standing-over-there`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** adding an -ing description straight after the thing described · **Role play targets:** standing over there / sitting next to / waiting outside

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — standing over there**: The -ing phrase goes straight after the noun, with no 'who' and no 'is'. 'The man who is standing there' becomes 'the man standing there'. _Examples:_ The man standing over there is my uncle. / The woman sitting by the window works here.
- **p2 — sitting next to**: This works when the description happens at the same time as the main sentence. Use a past participle for a passive or resulting-state meaning: the man seated at the front. _Examples:_ The person sitting next to you is the manager. / The child playing outside is theirs.
- **p3 — waiting outside**: The phrase must sit next to the noun it describes, or it attaches to the wrong thing. 'I saw the man waiting outside' means the man was waiting, not you. _Examples:_ The people waiting outside have been there an hour. / I spoke to the driver waiting outside.

## 32. Both of Which Were Late  `b2-both-of-which-were-late`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** quantity words inside a joined-up sentence · **Role play targets:** both of which / all of whom / some of which

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — both of which**: 'Of which' refers back to things: two trains, both of which were late. The clause adds extra information, so a comma comes before it. _Examples:_ I took two trains, both of which were late. / She wrote three books, all of which sold well.
- **p2 — all of whom**: 'Of whom' refers back to people and stays formal. In casual speech people say 'and all of them' as two sentences instead. _Examples:_ We invited forty people, all of whom came. / There were six candidates, none of whom were suitable.
- **p3 — some of which**: Any quantity word fits: some, several, half, most, neither. This pattern belongs to careful writing rather than everyday conversation. _Examples:_ He gave five reasons, some of which made sense. / They sent ten samples, most of which were damaged.

## 54. Yes, But Then Again  `b2-yes-but-then-again`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** granting a point before making your own · **Role play targets:** fair enough but / that said / having said that

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — fair enough but**: 'Fair enough, but...' grants the point briefly before countering. The concession must be genuine or the counter sounds dismissive. _Examples:_ Fair enough, but it still costs more. / Fair enough, but that wasn't the plan.
- **p2 — that said**: 'That said' introduces a qualification to what you have just agreed. It usually starts a new sentence. _Examples:_ That said, I'd still check first. / That said, it's not my decision.

## 55. Pros and Cons  `b2-pros-and-cons`

- **p1 — on the plus side**: 'On the plus side' introduces the advantages, usually after the problem. 'The upside is' means the same thing. _Examples:_ On the plus side, it's cheaper. / On the plus side, we'd have more space.
- **p2 — downside is**: 'The downside is' names the main disadvantage in one phrase. Naming one clear downside is more persuasive than listing five. _Examples:_ The downside is the distance. / The downside is that nobody knows us there.

## 56. Changing Your Mind  `b2-changing-your-mind`
