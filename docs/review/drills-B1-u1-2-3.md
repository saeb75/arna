# GlotMate — B1 units 1, 2, 3: practice block review

## Instructions for the reviewer

You are reviewing exercise content for **GlotMate**, an app that teaches English to adult beginners through conversation with a tutor avatar. Each lesson has three parts:

1. **Lecture** — the tutor explains the teaching points (shown below as "Teaching points").
2. **Practice block** — tap-based exercises, no keyboard (shown below). Every item has exactly ONE correct answer, graded by code (no AI).
3. **Role play** — free conversation with a character; the character's first line is shown below as "Role play openings" (one per learner context: everyday, work, travel, academic, exam).

**Exercise types:** Fill the gap (choose the word) · Arrange the words (build the sentence from tiles; extra tiles are distractors) · Read the situation, choose · Tap the wrong word · Complete the chat · Match the pairs · Listen and choose (audio plays, choose what you heard) · Listen and arrange · Say this sentence (speech recognition).

**Rules the content must follow:**
- **Exactly one correct answer.** No distractor may also be acceptable in the given context. This is the most important check.
- **Level B1.** Words and grammar a B1 learner can handle; only what the lesson teaches plus very common words.
- **Coverage:** each teaching point (p1, p2…) has at least 3 items; lessons with 2+ points end with at least 2 mixed ("mix") items.
- Distractors should be plausible learner mistakes, not nonsense.
- "Arrange" items: only one correct word order. "Listen and choose": options must sound different enough to tell apart.
- "Say this sentence": no names that speech recognition could misspell.
- "Tap the wrong word": only ONE word can fix the sentence (no subject–verb agreement errors, where changing the subject would also work).
- "Arrange the words": the first tile keeps its capital and the last tile keeps the final . ? ! — so a statement cannot be rebuilt as a question.
- Anything a drill tests (and every role play target phrase) must be taught in the teaching points.
- Role play openings: very simple, end with a question, and invite the learner to use the lesson's target phrases right away. The role play is voice only: an opening may ask about the learner's OWN life or things (they can imagine them), but never about a picture or object only the character can see.

**What to return:** a list of concrete problems only (skip items that are fine). For each: lesson id, the item (quote it), the problem, and your suggested fix. Use these categories: AMBIGUOUS (more than one correct answer), WRONG (the marked answer is not correct), LEVEL (too hard / unnatural for B1), WEAK (works but could teach better), COVERAGE (a teaching point is under-practised), OPENING (role play opening issue). ## 1. It Had Already Started  `b1-it-had-already-started`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** past perfect for an event that happened before another past event · **Role play targets:** it had already / I had never / by then we had

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — had + past participle**: Use 'had' plus the past participle to form the past perfect. The past perfect shows that one past event happened before another past event. The later past event usually uses the past simple. _Examples:_ I had finished my homework before I went out. / She had already left when I arrived.
- **p2 — already, never, by then + past perfect**: Words like 'already', 'never', and 'by then' are often used with the past perfect. 'Already' shows something happened before another past event. 'Never' means not at any time before that past moment. 'By then' means 'at or before that past moment'. _Examples:_ It had already started when I got there. / I had never seen that movie before last night. / By then we had eaten all the cake.

## 2. By the Time I Got There  `b1-by-the-time-i-got-there`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** past perfect with by the time, already and just · **Role play targets:** by the time / had just left / we had already

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — past perfect**: Use had + past participle to show one past action happened before another. The past perfect often comes with time words like by the time, already, and just. _Examples:_ By the time we arrived, the bus had left. / We had already finished dinner when he called.
- **p2 — by the time**: 'By the time' plus the past simple names a past moment; the past perfect in the other part shows what was already complete at that moment. The past perfect usually goes in the other part of the sentence. _Examples:_ By the time she got to the station, the train had just left. / By the time we noticed, they had already gone.
- **p3 — already and just**: Already means something happened before another event, often earlier than expected. Just means something happened a short time before another event. 'Already' and 'just' usually go between 'had' and the past participle: had just left. _Examples:_ We had already locked the door when she arrived. / He had just left when I called.

## 3. Setting the Scene  `b1-setting-the-scene`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** past continuous and past simple working together in a story · **Role play targets:** I was walking / when I saw / while we were

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — past continuous**: Use was or were plus verb-ing to show an action in progress in the past. Use 'was' with I, he, she and it; use 'were' with you, we and they. _Examples:_ I was walking to the store. / They were talking on the phone.
- **p2 — past simple**: Use the past form of the verb to show a finished action in the past. The past simple often interrupts the past continuous in a story. _Examples:_ I saw my friend. / She called me.
- **p3 — using both tenses together**: Use past continuous for the background action and past simple for the action that interrupts. 'While' usually introduces the longer background action in the past continuous; 'when' often introduces the short action in the past simple. _Examples:_ I was walking when I saw an accident. / While we were eating, the phone rang.

## 4. What Had Happened Before  `b1-what-had-happened-before`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** past perfect in questions and negatives · **Role play targets:** had you ever / I hadn't / what had happened

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — Had + subject + past participle?**: Use 'had' before the subject to make a question in the past perfect. The main verb is in the past participle form. Use past perfect questions to ask if something happened before another past event: Had you ever...? A question word comes first. In 'What had happened?', 'what' is the subject, so no other subject is needed. _Examples:_ Had you ever seen that movie before last night? / Had they finished dinner when you arrived? / What had happened before the police arrived?
- **p2 — subject + had not + past participle**: Use 'had not' or 'hadn't' before the past participle to make a negative in the past perfect. Do not add 'did' or change the past participle. Use past perfect negatives to say something did not happen before another past event. _Examples:_ I hadn't heard the news before you told me. / She had not finished her homework when the phone rang.

## 5. Suddenly, Meanwhile, Later  `b1-suddenly-meanwhile-later`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** expressions that move a story from one moment to the next · **Role play targets:** all of a sudden / not long after / in the meantime

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — all of a sudden**: Use 'all of a sudden' to show that something happened very quickly and unexpectedly. This phrase introduces a surprising or sudden event in the story. _Examples:_ All of a sudden, the bus stopped in the middle of the road. / We were walking, and all of a sudden, it started to rain.
- **p2 — not long after**: Use 'not long after' to show that something happened soon after another event. This phrase connects two events that are close in time. It can start a clause (Not long after we left, ...) or stand alone (Not long after, ...). _Examples:_ Not long after we left, we saw a beautiful mountain. / The train arrived, and not long after, we found our seats.
- **p3 — in the meantime**: Use 'in the meantime' to talk about what happens in the time between two events, often while you wait. It often introduces what you do while you wait for something else. _Examples:_ We missed the bus. In the meantime, we got some coffee. / The car was being fixed. In the meantime, we walked around the city.

## 8. Have You Finished Yet?  `b1-have-you-finished-yet`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** choosing between present perfect and past simple by time period · **Role play targets:** have you finished / I did that / not yet

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — present perfect for unfinished time**: Use present perfect for actions in a time period that is not finished (like today, this week, this year). **Type:** phrases · **Focus:** fixing plans with other people and confirming details · **Role play targets:** shall we say / does that suit you / let's pencil it in

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — shall we say**: Use 'shall we say' to suggest a possible time, date, or place for a plan. It is a polite way to make a suggestion and invite agreement. Here 'say' means 'suggest', and the time or place comes after it: Shall we say ten o'clock? _Examples:_ Shall we say 7 pm at the cafe? / We could meet next week. Shall we say Friday?
- **p2 — does that suit you**: Use 'does that suit you' to check if the suggested plan is convenient for the other person. It is a polite way to ask if someone is available or happy with the plan. _Examples:_ We can meet at 6 pm. Does that suit you? / How about Saturday afternoon? Does that suit you?
- **p3 — let's pencil it in**: Use 'let's pencil it in' to tentatively agree to a plan, with the idea that it might change. It means to make a plan, but not confirm it 100%. _Examples:_ Let's pencil it in for next Thursday. / If everyone agrees, let's pencil it in.

## 17. Might, May, Could  `b1-might-may-could`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** talking about future possibility that is still open · **Role play targets:** it might / we could always / there may be

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — it might + base verb**: 'It might' shows that something is possible in the future but not certain. Use 'it might' before the base form of a verb to talk about a possible action. _Examples:_ It might rain tomorrow. / It might be a good idea to call her.
- **p2 — we could always + base verb**: 'We could always' is used to suggest an option for the future that is not decided yet. Use 'we could always' before the base form of a verb to offer a possible idea. Here 'always' does not mean 'every time'; it shows one possible option. _Examples:_ We could always go to the park if the weather is nice. / If the train is late, we could always take a taxi.
- **p3 — there may be + noun**: 'There may be' is used to say that something could exist or happen in the future. Use 'there may be' before a noun to talk about possible things or events. _Examples:_ There may be more tickets available later. / There may be a meeting next week.

## 18. The Year Ahead  `b1-the-year-ahead`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** talking about your plans, hopes and worries for the coming year · **Role play targets:** I'm hoping to / I'd really like / if all goes well

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I'm hoping to**: Use 'I'm hoping to' to talk about something you want to do in the future. It shows a plan you want, but you are not sure it will happen. Follow 'I'm hoping to' with the base form of a verb. _Examples:_ I'm hoping to travel more this year. / I'm hoping to start a new job soon.
- **p2 — I'd really like**: Use 'I'd really like' to express a strong wish or hope for the future. This phrase is often used for dreams or important goals. It is followed by 'to' plus the base verb, or by a noun: I'd really like a holiday. _Examples:_ I'd really like to learn a new language. / I'd really like to spend more time with my family.
- **p3 — if all goes well**: Use 'if all goes well' to talk about your plans or hopes when you are not sure what will happen. This phrase shows that you hope things will work out, but there may be problems. It is often used at the beginning or end of a sentence about the future. _Examples:_ If all goes well, I'll finish my studies this year. / I'll move to a new city, if all goes well.
