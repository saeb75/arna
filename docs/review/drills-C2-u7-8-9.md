## 43. Talking About Ideas  `c2-talking-about-ideas`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** the vocabulary of abstraction and generalisation · **Role play targets:** in principle at least / broadly speaking / at a fundamental level

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — in principle at least**: 'In principle, at least' accepts the idea while leaving practice open. 'At least' makes the limit explicit: you accept the idea, not yet the practice. _Examples:_ In principle, at least, it should work. / In principle at least, they are equivalent.
- **p2 — broadly speaking**: 'Broadly speaking' marks a claim as a generalisation, so isolated exceptions do not automatically refute it. It also weakens the claim, so it is a poor choice where you do have the precise figure. _Examples:_ Broadly speaking, costs have risen. / Broadly speaking, that holds.
- **p3 — at a fundamental level**: 'At a fundamental level' claims the difference is not a matter of detail. It is a strong claim, so unless the context already makes it clear, name the fundamental difference afterwards. _Examples:_ At a fundamental level, they differ. / At a fundamental level, it is a question of trust.

**Practice block (12 items)**

1. `p1` _Read the situation, choose_ — You think a four-day week works as an idea, but you doubt it would work in your office. What do you say? → **✔ In principle, at least, it should work.** · It works, full stop. · Broadly speaking, it never works.
2. `p1` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: Do you think the plan will work? / YOU: I like the idea, but I doubt the details. / THEM: So do you support it or not? / YOU: ___ → **✔ In principle, at least. In practice, I'm not sure.** · Yes, in every way. · No, not even as an idea.
3. `p1` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "In principle, at least, it should work." → **✔ In principle, at least, it should work.** · In practice, it already works. · In the end, it didn't work.
4. `p1` _Read the situation, choose_ — You accept a rule as an idea only, and you want nobody to hear it as full support. Which version makes that limit explicit? → **✔ In principle, at least, it's fair.** · It's fair in practice too. · It's fair in every way.
5. `p2` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: Prices went down in March, though. So you're wrong that they've risen. / YOU: ___ → **✔ One month doesn't refute it. Broadly speaking, prices have risen.** · Broadly speaks, prices have risen. · Broad speaking, prices have risen.
6. `p2` _Read the situation, choose_ — Most of your friends found jobs quickly, but two did not. You want a general claim that those two do not disprove. What do you say? → **✔ Broadly speaking, my friends found work quickly.** · All of my friends found work quickly. · None of my friends found work quickly.
7. `p2` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **Broadly speaking, costs have risen.**
8. `p3` _Read the situation, choose_ — You think two schools differ in their basic aims, not in details, and you say what the difference is. What do you say? → **✔ At a fundamental level, they differ: one trains, one educates.** · At a fundamental level, they differ. · They differ slightly in the details.
9. `p3` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "At a fundamental level, they differ." → **✔ At a fundamental level, they differ.** · At a later stage, they agree. · On a personal level, they differ.
10. `p3` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: You said the two plans differ in their basic aims. How would you put it? / YOU: ___ → **✔ At a fundamental level, they differ: one trusts people, one controls them.** · They differ only in small details. · At a fundamentally level, they differ.

At a fundamental level, it's about the colour of the buses. · Broadly speaking, it never works and nobody pays.
12. `mix` _Match the pairs_ — In principle, at least ↔ the idea works; practice is open · Broadly speaking ↔ a generalisation with exceptions · At a fundamental level ↔ not a matter of detail

## 44. Defining the Undefinable  `c2-defining-the-undefinable`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** pinning down a concept that resists a clean definition · **Role play targets:** it resists definition / closest I can get / we'd know it when

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — it resists definition**: 'It resists definition' claims the difficulty is in the concept, not in your vocabulary. It only holds if you then try: said instead of attempting, it is an excuse. _Examples:_ It resists definition, but I'll try. / The word itself resists definition.
- **p2 — closest I can get**: 'The closest I can get is X' offers a definition while admitting it is approximate. Approximation is honest here, because a confident definition of a contested concept invites easy counter-examples. _Examples:_ The closest I can get is 'earned trust'. / The closest I can get is this.
- **p3 — we'd know it when**: 'We'd know it when we saw it' claims shared recognition where shared definition is missing. Recognition gives no shared test, and it fails when two people recognise different things. _Examples:_ We'd know it when we saw it. / We'd know it when it was gone.

**Practice block (11 items)**

1. `p1` _Read the situation, choose_ — Someone asks you to define 'fairness'. The concept is hard, not your English, and you will still try. What do you say? → **✔ It resists definition, but I'll try.** · I don't know that word in English. · It resists definition, so I won't try.
2. `p1` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: What is 'home', exactly? Can you define it? / YOU: ___ → **✔ It resists definition, but here's my attempt: where you're missed.** · It resist definition, but here's my attempt: where you're missed. · It resisting definition, but here's my attempt: where you're missed.
3. `p1` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "The word itself resists definition." → **✔ The word itself resists definition.** · This word is easy to define. · The rest of it needs no explanation.
4. `p2` _Read the situation, choose_ — You offer a definition of 'trust' but want to admit it is only approximate. What do you say? → **✔ The closest I can get is 'earned confidence'.** · The exact definition is 'earned confidence'. · The closer I can get is 'earned confidence'.
5. `p2` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: So what is 'integrity', in one phrase? / YOU: ___ → **✔ The closest I can get is acting the same when nobody checks.** · The closest I can getting is acting the same when nobody checks. · The close I can get is acting the same when nobody checks.
6. `p2` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **The closest I can get is earned trust.**
7. `p3` _Read the situation, choose_ — You can't define 'good taste', but you're sure you would both recognise it. What do you say? → **✔ We'd know it when we saw it.** · We'd knew it when we saw it. · We know it before we see it.
8. `p3` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: Two of us could recognise different things, though. / YOU: ___ → **✔ True. Recognition gives us no shared test.** · True. That makes recognition stronger than a definition. · True. So we'd never knows it.
9. `p3` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **We'd know it when we saw it.**
10. `mix` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: Can you define 'courage' for me? / YOU: ___ → **✔ It resists definition, but the closest I can get is fear plus action.** · It resists definition, so I won't even try. · The closest I can get is that I can't define it at all.
11. `mix` _Read the situation, choose_ — You admit 'success' is hard to define, offer an approximation, and add that people still recognise it. What do you say? → **✔ The closest I can get is 'enough'. We'd know it when we saw it.** · Success is exactly 'money'. Nobody could disagree. · It resists definition, so let's not talk about it.

## 45. Ethics and Trade-offs  `c2-ethics-and-trade-offs`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** discussing a dilemma where every option costs something · **Role play targets:** there's no clean choice / someone pays either way / I'd weigh it as

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — there's no clean choice**: 'There's no clean choice' rules out the costless option before anyone offers one. It is honest when you then choose; used by the person who must decide to avoid deciding, it is fence-sitting. _Examples:_ There's no clean choice here. / There's no clean choice, only a least bad one.
- **p2 — someone pays either way**: 'Someone pays either way' names that the cost moves rather than disappears. Unless the context already makes it clear, say who, or the phrase becomes a way of dismissing the cost. _Examples:_ Someone pays either way. / Someone pays either way — the question is who.
- **p3 — I'd weigh it as**: 'I'd weigh it as X' states how you actually rate an option: the smaller harm, a net gain. Making your assessment explicit is what lets others challenge it, rather than guess at it. _Examples:_ I'd weigh it as the smaller harm. / I'd weigh it as a net gain for most people.

**Practice block (11 items)**

3. `p1` _Tap the wrong word_ — There's no cleanly choice, only a least bad one. → wrong word: **cleanly** → There's no clean choice, only a least bad one.
4. `p2` _Read the situation, choose_ — Moving the bus depot saves money, but the cost moves to the people who now walk further. What do you say? → **✔ Someone pays either way: the people who walk further.** · Nobody pays: the cost simply disappears. · Someone pay either way: the people who walk further.
5. `p2` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: Someone pays either way. So what? / YOU: ___ → **✔ So we should say who. Here, it's the night workers.** · So the cost doesn't matter at all. · So nobody needs to know who.

7. `p3` _Read the situation, choose_ — You think the new bus route helps more people than it hurts, and you want your assessment open to argument. What do you say? → **✔ I'd weigh it as a net gain for most people.** · Obviously it's perfect. Next question. · I'd weighs it as a net gain for most people.

## 46. Technology and Society  `c2-technology-and-society`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** discussing a broad contemporary question from several angles · **Role play targets:** it cuts both ways / we're already seeing / hard to reverse

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — it cuts both ways**: 'It cuts both ways' claims one mechanism produces effects in both directions. Where the effects are not already clear, name both directions, or it is a way of appearing balanced without being it. _Examples:_ It cuts both ways. / Automation cuts both ways.
- **p2 — we're already seeing**: 'We're already seeing X' moves a claim from prediction to observation. That is a much stronger claim, so it fails badly if the thing is not in fact observable yet. _Examples:_ We're already seeing it in hiring. / We're already seeing the first effects.
- **p3 — hard to reverse**: 'Hard to reverse' shifts attention from immediate harm to lock-in and irreversibility. It supports caution even where the effect might be good, as long as it is clear what would make reversal difficult. _Examples:_ Once the system is embedded, it may be hard to reverse. / That kind of institutional change is hard to reverse.

## 47. Art and Taste  `c2-art-and-taste`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** defending a judgement that cannot be proved · **Role play targets:** it does something to me / I can't defend it / does that matter

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — it does something to me**: 'It does something to me' reports an effect, which is a fact about you rather than the work. Reporting the effect is defensible where a verdict about the work is not. _Examples:_ It does something to me I can't name. / That one does something to me.
- **p2 — I can't defend it**: 'I can't defend it' concedes that you cannot argue for it, not that no defence exists. Conceding that you cannot argue for it is stronger than a bad argument, which invites easy refutation. _Examples:_ I can't defend it, and I don't want to. / I can't defend it on any grounds.
- **p3 — does that matter**: 'Does that matter?' questions whether taste needs justification at all. It is a real question, and answering 'no' commits you to accepting other people's tastes on the same terms. _Examples:_ Does that matter, though? / Does that matter for a preference?

**Practice block (11 items)**

## 48. What Makes a Good Life  `c2-what-makes-a-good-life`

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — for me it comes down**: 'For me it comes down to X' reduces a large question to one thing you will actually defend. 'For me' keeps it a report of your view rather than a claim about everyone's. _Examples:_ For me it comes down to work I care about. / For me it comes down to two things.

## 49. Disagreeing About Values  `c2-disagreeing-about-values`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** a disagreement where the gap is fundamental, not factual · **Role play targets:** we start from different / I don't expect to / we'll have to differ

**Teaching points**

## 63. Being Interviewed  `c2-being-interviewed`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** answering well with no preparation and no script · **Role play targets:** good of you to ask / I suppose the honest / let me think

- **p1 — good of you to ask**: 'Good of you to ask' buys a moment as courtesy, though in an interview it can sound arch or faintly superior. - **p2 — I suppose the honest**: 'I suppose the honest answer is X' signals you are about to say the less polished thing. ## 64. A Conversation That Matters  `c2-a-conversation-that-matters`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** a conversation with real stakes and real feeling · **Role play targets:** I need to say / this is hard for me / thank you for listening

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I need to say**: 'There's something I need to say' announces the weight before the content. The announcement stops the other person answering the first half of a sentence you have not finished. _Examples:_ There's something I need to say. / I may say this badly, but I need to say it.
- **p2 — this is hard for me**: 'This is hard for me' explains your delivery, which stops hesitation being read as doubt. It is about you, so it should be brief: at length it asks the other person to manage your feelings. _Examples:_ This is hard for me. / This is hard for me, so bear with the wording.
- **p3 — thank you for listening**: 'Thank you for listening' closes on what the other person did rather than on the outcome. It works because a conversation like this often has no outcome to close on. _Examples:_ Thank you for listening. / Thank you for listening to all that.

## 65. Just Talking  `c2-just-talking`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** unstructured conversation with no agenda at all · **Role play targets:** no idea honestly / isn't that funny / anyway how are you

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — no idea honestly**: 'No idea, honestly' is a complete and comfortable answer where a manufactured one would be worse. It only stays comfortable if you are relaxed about it; said tensely it reads as a refusal. _Examples:_ No idea, honestly. / No idea, honestly — never thought about it.
- **p2 — isn't that funny**: 'Isn't that funny?' shares a small observation and invites only a light response. It needs no real answer, which is what makes it suit a conversation going nowhere in particular. _Examples:_ Isn't that funny? / Isn't that funny, how that works.
- **p3 — anyway how are you**: 'Anyway, how are you?' hands the turn back without needing the last topic to have ended. It is a soft reset rather than a close, so it keeps the conversation open. _Examples:_ Anyway, how are you? / Anyway — how are you, really?
