## 26. Given That, It Follows  `c1-given-that-it-follows`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** signalling the logical steps of an argument · **Role play targets:** given that / it follows that / which means that

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — given that**: 'Given that' introduces a premise the speaker treats as established, or assumes for the argument. It does not prove the premise; it sets it aside so the reasoning can proceed. _Examples:_ Given that the deadline is fixed, we must cut scope. / Given that nobody objected, we proceeded.
- **p2 — it follows that**: 'It follows that' presents the conclusion as following directly from what has been established. If the listener can accept the premise but reasonably reject the conclusion, the phrase is too strong: state the missing premise first. _Examples:_ It follows that we need a second option. / It follows that the original plan will not work.
- **p3 — which means that**: 'Which means that' draws a consequence without claiming logical necessity. It is the softer of the two and fits most everyday reasoning. _Examples:_ The budget is frozen, which means that hiring stops. / She has resigned, which means that we are short-staffed.

**Practice block (11 items)**

1. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — ___ that the deadline is fixed, we must cut scope. → **✔ Given** · Giving · Give
2. `p1` _Read the situation, choose_ — Your team agrees the office closes in May. You build on that fact to say the move must start now. What do you say? → **✔ Given that the office closes in May, we must start the move now.** · Given than the office closes in May, we must start the move now. · Giving that the office closes in May, we must start the move now.
3. `p1` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "Given that nobody objected, we proceeded." → **✔ Given that nobody objected, we proceeded.** · Nobody objected, so we waited. · Given the chance, nobody would object.
4. `p2` _Fill the gap_ — We must start now, only five people are available, and the plan needs ten. It ___ that we need a smaller plan. → **✔ follows** · follow · following
5. `p2` _Tap the wrong word_ — It follows than we need a second option. → wrong word: **than** → It follows that we need a second option.
6. `p2` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: Production must continue, our only supplier has just closed, and we can't make the parts ourselves. / YOU: ___ → **✔ It follows that we need a new supplier.** · It follow that we need a new supplier. · It follows than we need a new supplier.
7. `p3` _Fill the gap_ — She has resigned, ___ that we are short-staffed. → **✔ which means** · which mean · what means
8. `p3` _Listen and arrange_ — 🔊 **The budget is frozen, which means that hiring stops.** (extra tiles: mean)
9. `p3` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **She has resigned, which means that we are short-staffed.**
10. `mix` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: Prices go up next week, and we are booking this trip either way. Why book today? / YOU: ___ → **✔ Given that prices go up next week, it follows that waiting until then will cost more.** · Given that prices go up next week, it follow that waiting until then will cost more. · Giving that prices go up next week, it follows that waiting until then will cost more.

## 27. Granted, However  `c1-granted-however`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** conceding a point in order to strengthen your own · **Role play targets:** granted that / I'll admit that / that notwithstanding

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — granted that**: 'Granted that' accepts a point without accepting the conclusion drawn from it. A concession that costs you nothing is usually spotted as a tactic. _Examples:_ Granted that the cost is high, the alternative is higher. / Granted that it is unusual, it is not unprecedented.
- **p2 — I'll admit that**: 'I'll admit that' concedes a point openly; whether it truly weakens your case depends on what you concede. Conceding a real weakness makes the rest of the case more credible. _Examples:_ I'll admit that the evidence is thin. / I'll admit that we underestimated the timeline.
- **p3 — that notwithstanding**: 'That notwithstanding' returns to your case after the concession. It is formal; 'even so' does the same work in ordinary speech. _Examples:_ That notwithstanding, the case still holds. / That notwithstanding, I would proceed.

**Practice block (11 items)**

1. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — ___ that the cost is high, the alternative is higher. → **✔ Granted** · Grant · Grants
2. `p1` _Read the situation, choose_ — A friend says your plan is expensive. You accept that point, but not their conclusion. What do you say? → **✔ Granted that it is expensive, it still saves money over time.** · Granted than it is expensive, it still saves money over time. · Grants that it is expensive, it still saves money over time.
3. `p1` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "Granted that it is risky, it is worth trying." → **✔ Granted that it is risky, it is worth trying.** · It is risky, so it is not worth trying. · Grant me one more day to try.
4. `p2` _Fill the gap_ — I'll ___ that we underestimated the timeline. → **✔ admit** · admitted · admitting
5. `p2` _Read the situation, choose_ — Your report's evidence comes from only two small surveys. You want to concede that real weakness. What do you say? → **✔ I'll admit that the evidence is thin.** · I'll admit that nothing is ever certain. · I'll admit that some people always complain.
6. `p2` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **I'll admit that the first version was too slow.**
7. `p3` _Fill the gap_ — The risks are real. That ___, I would still proceed. → **✔ notwithstanding** · notwithstand · notwithstandings
8. `p3` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: So you accept the plan has a weak point? / YOU: ___ → **✔ I do. That notwithstanding, the case still holds.** · I do. That notwithstand, the case still holds. · I do. That notwithstandings, the case still holds.
9. `p3` _Listen and arrange_ — 🔊 **That notwithstanding, I would proceed.** (extra tiles: notwithstand)
10. `mix` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: Your project is late and over budget. Why continue? / YOU: ___ → **✔ I'll admit that it is late. That notwithstanding, it is nearly finished.** · I'll admitted that it is late. That notwithstanding, it is nearly finished. · I'll admit that it is late. That notwithstand, it is nearly finished.
11. `mix` _Read the situation, choose_ — A colleague says your plan is risky. You agree that it is risky, then return to your case. Which is correct? → **✔ I'll admit that it's risky. That notwithstanding, it's our best option.** · I'll admit than it's risky. That notwithstanding, it's our best option. · I'll admit that it's risky. That notwithstandingly, it's our best option.

## 28. Correlation and Cause  `c1-correlation-and-cause`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** being careful about claiming that one thing caused another · **Role play targets:** that doesn't prove / it may simply reflect / correlation isn't causation

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — that doesn't prove**: 'That doesn't prove' rejects the inference, not the observation. Separating the two keeps the disagreement narrow and answerable. _Examples:_ That doesn't prove the policy caused it. / That doesn't prove one led to the other.
- **p2 — it may simply reflect**: 'It may simply reflect' offers a rival explanation for the same pattern. An alternative explanation shows the pattern is compatible with more than one cause. _Examples:_ It may simply reflect who was asked. / It may simply reflect the time of year.
- **p3 — correlation isn't causation**: 'Correlation isn't causation' names the error but does not explain why it applies here. A plausible alternative shows why the pattern alone does not establish causation. _Examples:_ Correlation isn't causation, after all. / Correlation isn't causation, as we both know.

**Practice block (11 items)**

1. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — The numbers rose after the policy, but that ___ prove the policy caused it. → **✔ doesn't** · don't · isn't
2. `p1` _Read the situation, choose_ — Sales rose after the new logo. You accept the figures but reject the idea that the logo caused the rise. What do you say? → **✔ The figures are right, but that doesn't prove the logo caused it.** · The figures are wrong, so the logo did nothing. · The figures are right, so the logo caused it.
3. `p1` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "That doesn't prove one led to the other." → **✔ That doesn't prove one led to the other.** · That proves one led to the other. · That doesn't matter to the others.
4. `p2` _Fill the gap_ — The survey looks positive, but it may simply ___ who was asked. → **✔ reflect** · reflects · reflected
5. `p2` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: Complaints doubled in December. The new staff must be worse. / YOU: ___ → **✔ It may simply reflect the holiday season.** · It may simply reflects the holiday season. · It may simple reflect the holiday season.
6. `p2` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **It may simply reflect the weather.**
7. `p3` _Fill the gap_ — Correlation isn't ___, after all. → **✔ causation** · causing · caused

10. `mix` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: People who own dogs are healthier. So dogs make people healthy. / YOU: ___ → **✔ That doesn't prove it. It may simply reflect who can afford a dog.** · That doesn't prove it. It may simply reflects who can afford a dog. · That don't prove it. It may simply reflect who can afford a dog.
11. `mix` _Read the situation, choose_ — Your manager says staff who work from home are more productive, so home working causes it. You name the error and offer another cause. What do you say? → **✔ Correlation isn't causation. It may simply reflect who chose to work from home.** · Correlation isn't causation. It may simply reflected who chose to work from home. · Correlation isn't causing. It may simply reflect who chose to work from home.

## 29. The Counterargument  `c1-the-counterargument`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** stating the opposing view fairly before answering it · **Role play targets:** one might argue / critics would say / against that

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — one might argue**: 'One might argue' introduces the opposing case in neutral language. Stating it weakly is easy to spot and damages your credibility. _Examples:_ One might argue that the cost outweighs the benefit. / One might argue the opposite entirely.
- **p2 — critics would say**: 'Critics would say' attributes the objection to a general opposing group. To avoid a straw man, the objection must still be one critics actually make. _Examples:_ Critics would say this rewards failure. / Critics would say we have tried this before.
- **p3 — against that**: 'Against that' marks the point where you begin your answer. Answering before the objection is fully stated looks evasive. _Examples:_ Against that, the evidence points the other way. / Against that, the cost of inaction is higher.

**Practice block (11 items)**

1. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — One might ___ that the cost outweighs the benefit. → **✔ argue** · argues · arguing
2. `p1` _Read the situation, choose_ — You support a four-day week. You introduce the opposing case in neutral language. What do you say? → **✔ One might argue that it would hurt customers.** · Only a fool would argue that it would hurt customers. · Nobody could seriously argue that it would hurt customers.
3. `p1` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **One might argue that it is too early.**
4. `p2` _Fill the gap_ — Critics would ___ this rewards failure. → **✔ say** · says · said
5. `p2` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: What will opponents of the plan say? / YOU: ___ → **✔ Critics would say we have tried this before.** · Critics would says we have tried this before. · Critic would say we have tried this before.
6. `p2` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "Critics would say this rewards failure." → **✔ Critics would say this rewards failure.** · Critics say this is a failure. · We should reward success, not failure.
7. `p3` _Fill the gap_ — Critics would say it is expensive. ___ that, the cost of doing nothing is higher. → **✔ Against** · Again · Along
8. `p3` _Read the situation, choose_ — You have stated the objection fully. Now you begin your answer. What do you say? → **✔ Against that, the evidence points the other way.** · Against to that, the evidence points the other way. · Against that, the evidence point the other way.

Against that, it is far cheaper.** · Critics would say it is too slow. Against that, it far cheaper. · Critics would says it is too slow. Against that, it is far cheaper.
11. `mix` _Read the situation, choose_ — You want to state an objection neutrally and then answer it. Which is correct? → **✔ One might argue that it is risky. Against that, doing nothing is riskier.** · One might argues that it is risky. Against that, doing nothing is riskier. · One might argue that it is risky. Against of that, doing nothing is riskier.

## 30. Anticipating Objections  `c1-anticipating-objections`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** arguing while pre-empting the reply you expect · **Role play targets:** you might object that / some will say / I'd answer that by

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — you might object that**: 'You might object that' raises the objection in the other person's voice. Raising it first shows you have considered it rather than missed it. _Examples:_ You might object that this has been tried. / You might object that the sample is small.
- **p2 — some will say**: 'Some will say' raises a common objection without attributing it to the listener. This avoids putting words in the other person's mouth. _Examples:_ Some will say it is too early. / Some will say the risk is unacceptable.
- **p3 — I'd answer that by**: 'I'd answer that by pointing to...' gives the answer a shape before the content. Announcing the shape of the answer stops it sounding improvised. _Examples:_ I'd answer that by pointing to the pilot. / I'd answer that by separating the two cases.

**Practice block (11 items)**

You might object that the sample is small.** · We start with one site. You might objected that the sample is small. · We start with one site. You might object than the sample is small.
3. `p1` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "You might object that the sample is small." → **✔ You might object that the sample is small.** · You might expect the sample to be small. · You must buy a bigger sample.

## 31. A Structured Case  `c1-a-structured-case`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** arguing a position from opening to conclusion · **Role play targets:** my first point / which brings me to / for these reasons

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — my first point**: Numbering the parts shows that the case has an order and that more is coming. It also makes it obvious if you never reach the second point. _Examples:_ My first point is about cost. / My first point concerns timing.
- **p2 — which brings me to**: 'Which brings me to' links one part to the next rather than just stopping. The link shows how the parts connect; the content still has to do the arguing. _Examples:_ Which brings me to the second issue. / Which brings me to my main concern.
- **p3 — for these reasons**: 'For these reasons' ties the parts to the conclusion. A conclusion that does not refer back to the parts leaves them unused. _Examples:_ For these reasons, I would delay. / For these reasons, the proposal should be rejected.

**Practice block (11 items)**

1. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — My ___ point is about cost. → **✔ first** · firsts · firstly

3. `p1` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "My first point is about cost." → **✔ My first point is about cost.** · My last point is about cost. · My first job was in a cafe.
4. `p2` _Fill the gap_ — That covers the cost, ___ brings me to the second issue. → **✔ which** · what · who
5. `p2` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: So that's the cost. What's next? / YOU: ___ → **✔ Which brings me to the second problem: timing.** · Which bring me to the second problem: timing. · Which brings me at the second problem: timing.
6. `p2` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **Which brings me to the second issue.**
7. `p3` _Fill the gap_ — For these ___, the proposal should be rejected. → **✔ reasons** · reason · reasoning
8. `p3` _Read the situation, choose_ — You have made three points. Now you tie them to your conclusion. What do you say? → **✔ For these reasons, I would delay.** · For this reasons, I would delay. · For these reasons, I would delayed.

## 32. Defending Under Pressure  `c1-defending-under-pressure`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** holding up when your argument is challenged hard · **Role play targets:** I stand by that / that misses my point / let me clarify

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I stand by that**: 'I stand by that' refuses to retreat without escalating the exchange. It works only if you then give a reason; alone it sounds stubborn. _Examples:_ I stand by that, and here is why. / I stand by the figure I gave.
- **p2 — that misses my point**: 'That misses my point' names a misreading rather than a disagreement. Answering a misreading as though it were an objection concedes ground you never lost. _Examples:_ That misses my point, which was about timing. / That misses what I actually claimed.
- **p3 — let me clarify**: 'Let me clarify' restates the position in different words. Repeating the same words rarely resolves a misunderstanding. _Examples:_ Let me clarify what I mean by that. / Let me clarify the distinction.

3. `p1` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "I stand by the figure I gave." → **✔ I stand by the figure I gave.** · I can't stand the figure I gave. · I stood by the window and waited.
4. `p2` _Read the situation, choose_ — Someone says you claimed the plan can never work. You only said it would not work this year. What do you say? → **✔ That misses my point, which was about this year.** · That is a fair objection, and I accept it. · I stand by that: it can never work.

## 41. Hedged and Blunt  `c1-hedged-and-blunt`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** how much distance to put between yourself and a claim · **Role play targets:** it would appear / one could argue / quite frankly

## 42. Tends To, Has a Habit Of  `c1-tends-to-has-a-habit-of`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** describing tendencies with the right degree of commitment · **Role play targets:** tends to / has a habit of / more often than not

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — tends to**: 'Tends to' describes a pattern without claiming it holds every time. It is neutral: it carries no approval or disapproval on its own. _Examples:_ The system tends to slow down in the afternoon. / He tends to arrive late.
- **p2 — has a habit of**: 'Has a habit of' usually carries mild disapproval. The same fact stated with 'tends to' sounds neutral by comparison. _Examples:_ He has a habit of interrupting. / She has a habit of leaving things until the last minute.
- **p3 — more often than not**: 'More often than not' means in a majority of cases, without specifying the proportion. It states a majority explicitly, where 'usually' is read very differently by different listeners. _Examples:_ More often than not, it works. / More often than not, nobody replies.

## 45. Choosing the Register  `c1-choosing-the-register`

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — formally I'd say**: The formal version avoids contractions and colloquialisms; clear verbs still beat unnecessary nominalisation. 'I am unable to attend' rather than 'I can't come'. _Examples:_ Formally I'd say: I am unable to attend. / Formally I'd say we are unable to proceed.
