## 46. Diplomatic Disagreement  `c1-diplomatic-disagreement`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** objecting clearly without damaging the relationship · **Role play targets:** I see it differently / with respect I'd argue / I'm not entirely convinced

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I see it differently**: 'I see it differently' locates the disagreement in perspective rather than in error. It leaves both positions standing, which keeps the exchange open. _Examples:_ I see it differently, and here is why. / I see it differently on the timing.
- **p2 — with respect I'd argue**: 'With respect' signals disagreement with someone senior or in public. In British English the tone matters: said flatly it can read as sharp. _Examples:_ With respect, I'd argue the opposite. / With respect, I'd argue the data says otherwise.
- **p3 — I'm not entirely convinced**: 'I'm not entirely convinced' withholds agreement without asserting the opposite. 'Entirely' is what softens it; without that word it lands much harder. _Examples:_ I'm not entirely convinced by that. / I'm not entirely convinced the risk is covered.

**Practice block (11 items)**

1. `p1` _Read the situation, choose_ — Your manager says the launch should be in May. You think June is better, and you want to show it is a matter of view, not a mistake. What do you say? → **✔ I see it differently, and here is why.** · I see differently it, and here is why. · I see it in a differently way, and here is why.
2. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — I see it ___ on the timing. → **✔ differently** · difference · differ
3. `p1` _Listen and arrange_ — 🔊 **I see it differently, and here is why.** (extra tiles: sees)
4. `p2` _Read the situation, choose_ — In a public meeting, a senior director says the plan is safe. You think the data shows the opposite. What do you say? → **✔ With respect, I'd argue the data says otherwise.** · With respect, I'd arguing the data says otherwise. · With respectful, I'd argue the data says otherwise.
5. `p2` _Fill the gap_ — With ___, I'd argue the opposite. → **✔ respect** · respected · respectful
6. `p2` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: As your director, I think we should cut the training budget. / YOU: ___ → **✔ With respect, I'd argue that is where we'll lose people.** · With respect, I'd to argue that is where we'll lose people. · With respect, I'd argued that is where we'll lose people.
7. `p3` _Read the situation, choose_ — A colleague says the new plan covers every risk. You doubt it, but you don't want to claim the opposite. What do you say? → **✔ I'm not entirely convinced it covers everything.** · I'm not entire convinced it covers everything. · I'm entirely not convinced it covers everything.
8. `p3` _Say this sentence_ — 🎤 **I'm not entirely convinced by that.**

## 47. Chairing a Discussion  `c1-chairing-a-discussion`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** running a conversation with several people in it · **Role play targets:** let's hear from / coming back to / we're running short

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — let's hear from**: 'Let's hear from' invites someone in by name, which is harder to decline than a general invitation. Naming the person also makes it less likely that the loudest voice fills the gap. _Examples:_ Let's hear from Maya on this. / Let's hear from someone who hasn't spoken.
- **p2 — coming back to**: 'Coming back to' returns to a point that was left, rather than cutting someone off. It frames the return as picking up rather than correcting. _Examples:_ Coming back to the budget question. / Coming back to what Ana raised earlier.
- **p3 — we're running short**: 'We're running short of time' manages the clock without blaming a speaker. Naming the constraint rather than the person keeps the room calm. _Examples:_ We're running short of time. / We're running short, so briefly if you can.

**Practice block (11 items)**

1. `p1` _Read the situation, choose_ — You are chairing a meeting. One colleague, Ana, has not said anything for twenty minutes. What do you say? → **✔ Let's hear from Ana on this.** · Let's hear Ana from on this. · Let's hearing from Ana on this.

3. `p1` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "Let's hear from someone who hasn't spoken." → **✔ Let's hear from someone who hasn't spoken.** · Let's hear from someone who has spoken twice. · Let's wait for someone who hasn't arrived.

What do you say? → **✔ Coming back to the budget question, where are we?** · Coming back the budget question, where are we? · Coming backs to the budget question, where are we?
5. `p2` _Complete the chat_ — THEM: Sorry, I think we've drifted a bit. Where were we? / YOU: ___ → **✔ Coming back to the staffing point.** · Coming backed to the staffing point. · Coming back of the staffing point.

## 48. Negotiating Position  `c1-negotiating-position`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** signalling where you can move and where you cannot · **Role play targets:** there's room on / that's not something we / what if we both

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — there's room on**: 'There's room on price' signals flexibility on one item only. Naming one flexible item can imply that the others are fixed, without saying so. _Examples:_ There's room on the timeline. / There's room on price, within limits.
- **p2 — that's not something we**: 'That's not something we can move on' marks a limit without ending the talk. 'We' rather than 'I' makes the limit institutional rather than personal. _Examples:_ That's not something we can move on. / That's not something we would consider.
- **p3 — what if we both**: 'What if we both...' proposes a mutual concession rather than a one-sided one. A mutual proposal frames the agreement as reciprocal because both sides concede something. _Examples:_ What if we both moved a little? / What if we both took part of the risk?

## 49. Delivering Bad News  `c1-delivering-bad-news`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** announcing something unwelcome clearly and humanely · **Role play targets:** I'm afraid we've decided / this wasn't easy / I want to be straight

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I'm afraid we've decided**: State the decision promptly and explain afterwards; leading with lengthy reasons can bury it. 'I'm afraid' acknowledges the news is unwelcome without hedging the content. _Examples:_ I'm afraid we've decided not to proceed. / I'm afraid we've decided to end the trial.
- **p2 — this wasn't easy**: 'This wasn't an easy decision' signals that it was considered carefully; on its own it does not acknowledge the impact on the listener. To acknowledge the impact you have to name it, so add what it will mean for them. _Examples:_ This wasn't an easy decision. / This wasn't easy and I want to explain it.
- **p3 — I want to be straight**: 'I want to be straight with you' signals that no further softening is coming. Following it with a hedge undermines the whole message. _Examples:_ I want to be straight with you about why. / I want to be straight: the funding has gone.

## 50. Giving Difficult Feedback  `c1-giving-difficult-feedback`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** a candid conversation that stays constructive · **Role play targets:** what I've observed is / how do you see it / I'd like us to

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — what I've observed is**: 'What I've observed is...' describes behaviour rather than judging the person. Behaviour can be changed; a character verdict leaves nowhere to go. _Examples:_ What I've observed is that deadlines slip. / What I've observed is a pattern, not one incident.
- **p2 — how do you see it**: 'How do you see it?' invites their account before you conclude. Asking after the observation but before the conclusion keeps the conversation two-way. _Examples:_ How do you see it? / How do you see it from where you sit?
- **p3 — I'd like us to**: 'I'd like us to agree on...' proposes a shared next step rather than issuing an instruction. 'Us' makes the follow-up joint rather than imposed. _Examples:_ I'd like us to agree on a check-in. / I'd like us to find a way through this.

## 51. A Negotiation  `c1-a-negotiation`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** reaching an agreement that neither side resents · **Role play targets:** I could live with / that's my limit / do we have a deal

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I could live with**: 'I could live with' accepts something without pretending to like it. Overstating your enthusiasm invites the other side to push further. _Examples:_ I could live with that. / I could live with the later date.
- **p2 — that's my limit**: 'That's my limit' is only useful if it is true; a bluff that is called costs you the next round. Stating a limit once, clearly, is stronger than repeating it. _Examples:_ That's my limit, honestly. / That's my limit on the timeline.
- **p3 — do we have a deal**: 'Do we have a deal?' closes explicitly rather than letting the talk drift. An unclosed negotiation tends to reopen at the worst moment. _Examples:_ So do we have a deal? / Do we have a deal on that basis?

## 52. Handling a Crisis Call  `c1-handling-a-crisis-call`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** staying clear and calm while information is still incomplete · **Role play targets:** here's what we know / what we're doing now / I'll update you

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — here's what we know**: 'Here's what we know' fences off the confirmed facts from everything else. The fence is what stops speculation being heard as fact. _Examples:_ Here's what we know so far. / Here's what we know, and what we don't.
- **p2 — what we're doing now**: 'What we're doing now' reports action, which is often what the listener needs most. Action can be reported honestly even when the cause is still unknown. _Examples:_ What we're doing now is checking every site. / What we're doing now is containing it.
- **p3 — I'll update you**: 'I'll update you at four' commits to a time rather than to an answer. Missing a promised update can damage trust further, even when there is still no final answer. _Examples:_ I'll update you at four. / I'll update you as soon as we know more.

**Practice block (11 items)**

1. `p1` _Read the situation, choose_ — A client calls during an outage. Three sites are down and the cause is unknown. How do you start? → **✔ Here's what we know: three sites are down.** · Here's what we knows: three sites are down. · Here's what do we know: three sites are down.
2. `p1` _Fill the gap_ — Here's what we ___ so far. → **✔ know** · known · knowing
3. `p1` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "Here's what we know, and what we don't." → **✔ Here's what we know, and what we don't.** · Here's what we knew before the outage. · We don't know anything yet.
