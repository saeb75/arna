| Lesson | Item (quoted) | Category | Problem and fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| 59 | “Be honest with me, but not brutal. What did you think of my new song?” | OPENING | The voice-only learner has not heard the character’s song, so there is no basis for an opinion. Ask about something the learner can imagine, e.g. “You’ve rewritten one of your songs. Is the new version slightly better?” |
| 62 | “She isn't rude. I'd call it honest.” | WEAK | “It” has no clear antecedent and sounds unnatural when describing a person. Use “Her comment wasn't rude. I'd call it honest.” |
| 62 | “Well, you could say she's very honest.” | WEAK | “Honest” is not a clear milder alternative to “rude”; it changes the meaning rather than carefully reframing it. Use the taught and more natural “Well, you could say she's very direct.” |
| 63 | “I have been working on this for weeks. What do you think?” | OPENING | “This” refers to work the learner cannot inspect in a voice-only role play. Supply an assessable fact, e.g. “My presentation is forty minutes long, but the limit is ten. Do you think it’s ready?” |
| 63 | “I have written this paragraph for my class. Be honest, is it any good?” | OPENING | The learner cannot see the paragraph. Give relevant information, e.g. “My paragraph has no examples, but I think it’s ready. Do you agree?” |
| 64 | “Would you be so kind as to call a taxi, if it's not too much trouble?” | WEAK | It stacks two strong politeness devices and sounds excessively formal, despite the lesson warning about stiffness. Split the requests: “Would you be so kind as to call a taxi? Could you ask it to wait, if it’s not too much trouble?” |
| 65 | “everyday: Emeka (a close colleague)…” / “work: Chiara (a flatmate)…” | OPENING | The learner contexts are reversed: a colleague belongs naturally under work, while a flatmate belongs under everyday. Swap these two labels and openings. |
| 66 | “Thank you for contacting us. How can I help?” / “We received your message. Could you confirm what you need?” / “Good morning. What is the nature of your enquiry?” | OPENING | These frame live conversations, in which “I am writing to…” and “Please find attached…” are unnatural. Frame them as email-composition tasks, e.g. “Imagine you are emailing us about group prices. How does your message begin?” |
| 67 | “THEM: Hi! Sorry, I haven't replied yet. / YOU: No problem, just checking you got my message.” | WEAK | The reply proves that the recipient got the message, making “checking you got my message” pragmatically pointless. Change THEM to “Hi—was there something you needed?” and answer “Just checking you got my message.” |
| 71 | “Can you talk us through what these numbers are doing?” | OPENING | “These numbers” refers to unseen information in a voice-only role play. State the data aloud, e.g. “Sales rose from 1,000 to 1,500, then stopped changing. Can you describe the trend?” |
| 76 | “Maps. I couldn't live without it.” | WRONG | Unless “Maps” is explicitly an app name, the plural noun requires “them.” Use “Maps. I couldn't live without them,” or “My maps app. I couldn't live without it.” |
| 76 | “The best thing about it is the battery.” | WEAK | For a smartwatch, the advantage is normally its battery life, not the battery itself. Use “The best thing about it is the battery life.” |
| 78 | “We are out of time. Can you pull it together for us?” | OPENING | The role play begins here, so the learner has no preceding discussion to summarise. Supply conclusions, e.g. “We agree the cost is too high, but not what to cut. Can you sum that up?” The other wrap-up openings need similar concrete content. |

General suggestions

- Replace references to unseen songs, writing, figures, or prior discussions with facts stated in the opening.
- Keep role-play settings aligned with their everyday, work, travel, academic, and exam labels.
- Avoid stacking several softeners unless the lesson explicitly demonstrates that the result can sound excessive.
