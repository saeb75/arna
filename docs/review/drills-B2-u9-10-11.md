## 57. Remember to Do, Remember Doing  `b2-remember-to-do-remember-doing`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** words whose meaning flips between to and -ing · **Role play targets:** remember to call / remember calling / I forgot to

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — remember to call**: 'Remember to do' is about a duty that comes after the remembering. Depending on the tense, the action may still be ahead or already done: did you remember to call her? _Examples:_ Remember to call her tomorrow. / Did you remember to lock the door?
- **p2 — remember calling**: 'Remember doing' is about a memory of something already done. 'I remember calling her' means the call happened and you recall it. _Examples:_ I remember calling her that evening. / I don't remember saying that.
- **p3 — I forgot to**: 'I forgot to lock it' means I did not do the intended action; it says nothing about the current state. 'I'll never forget meeting her' is a memory, not a duty. _Examples:_ I forgot to lock the door. / I'll never forget meeting her.

4. `p2` _Fill the gap_ — I clearly remember ___ the keys on the table last night. → **✔ leaving** · to leave · leave

6. `p2` _Listen and choose_ — 🔊 "I remember locking the door." → **✔ I remember locking the door.** · I remember the blue door. · I need to lock the door.

## 58. I Regret to Tell You  `b2-i-regret-to-tell-you`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** formal announcements with regret, be pleased and be sorry · **Role play targets:** I regret to / I'm pleased to / we're sorry to

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I regret to**: 'I regret to inform you' introduces bad news you are about to give. 'Regret informing' is different: it regrets an act of telling that already happened. _Examples:_ I regret to inform you that the post has been filled. / We regret to say the event is cancelled.
- **p2 — I'm pleased to**: 'I'm pleased to' and 'I'm delighted to' introduce good news. 'Delighted' is warmer and is common in offers and invitations. _Examples:_ I'm pleased to confirm your booking. / We're delighted to offer you the position.
- **p3 — we're sorry to**: 'We're sorry to' is softer than 'we regret to' and works in speech as well as writing. All three take 'to' plus a base verb; announcements usually use inform, say, confirm or announce, but others fit too. _Examples:_ We're sorry to say there is a delay. / We're sorry to hear about the problem.

## 59. Slightly, Fairly, Somewhat  `b2-slightly-fairly-somewhat`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** softening how strong a description is · **Role play targets:** slightly better / fairly good / somewhat unusual

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — slightly better**: 'Slightly' marks a very small difference or degree; it is very common with comparatives. 'Slightly better' means a little better, with only a small difference. _Examples:_ It's slightly better than the old one. / The second version is slightly clearer.
- **p2 — fairly good**: 'Fairly' usually gives a moderate assessment; its exact strength depends on the adjective and the tone. In British English 'quite' can express a similar moderate meaning, though its strength varies by context. _Examples:_ The results are fairly good. / It's fairly straightforward once you start.
- **p3 — somewhat unusual**: 'Somewhat' is more formal than 'fairly' and common in writing. 'A bit' and 'a little' are the casual equivalents. _Examples:_ The response was somewhat unusual. / The figures are somewhat misleading.

## 60. Nearly, Barely, Hardly  `b2-nearly-barely-hardly`

**Type:** grammar · **Focus:** near-negative words and what they quietly imply · **Role play targets:** barely enough / hardly any / nearly finished

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — barely enough**: 'Barely' means only just, and it carries a negative feeling on its own. Say 'there was barely enough', not 'there wasn't barely enough'. _Examples:_ There was barely enough time. / I could barely hear her.
- **p2 — hardly any**: 'Hardly any' means almost none, and 'hardly ever' means almost never. The verb after them stays positive because the negative is already there. _Examples:_ There were hardly any people. / We hardly ever see them now.
- **p3 — nearly finished**: 'Nearly' and 'almost' do not work as negatives; they mean something came close to happening. The feeling comes from the situation, not the word: we nearly crashed is alarming, I am nearly finished is not. _Examples:_ I'm nearly finished. / We almost missed it.

## 61. Close Enough  `b2-close-enough`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** approximating when you do not have exact facts · **Role play targets:** roughly speaking / somewhere around / give or take a bit

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — roughly speaking**: 'Roughly speaking' opens an approximate answer and warns the listener. It usually starts the sentence, before the figure. _Examples:_ Roughly speaking, about a third. / Roughly speaking, it takes an hour.
- **p2 — somewhere around**: 'Somewhere around' or 'somewhere in the region of' softens a figure. The second is more formal and common in writing. _Examples:_ Somewhere around fifty. / Somewhere in the region of two thousand.
- **p3 — give or take ten minutes**: 'Give or take' introduces the size of the possible margin: give or take ten minutes. It is informal and follows the figure; 'give or take a bit' leaves the margin vague. _Examples:_ An hour, give or take ten minutes. / Three hundred, give or take a bit.

## 62. Loaded Words  `b2-loaded-words`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** choosing between words that mean the same but feel different · **Role play targets:** I'd call it / you could say / that's putting it mildly

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I'd call it**: 'I'd call it...' offers a different word for the same thing. It reframes without directly contradicting the other person. _Examples:_ I'd call it careful rather than slow. / I'd call it firm, not rude.
- **p2 — you could say**: 'You could say' introduces a milder or harsher alternative. It concedes that both words fit the same facts. _Examples:_ You could say she's direct. / You could say it was a mistake.
- **p3 — that's putting it mildly**: 'That's putting it mildly' says the real situation is stronger than the word used. It comments on someone else's choice of word rather than the facts. _Examples:_ Difficult? That's putting it mildly. / That's putting it mildly, to be honest.

## 63. Saying It Kindly  `b2-saying-it-kindly`

**Type:** practice · **Focus:** delivering an unwelcome opinion without causing offence · **Role play targets:** I hope you don't mind / with respect / can I be frank

**Teaching points**

- **p1 — I hope you don't mind**: 'I hope you don't mind me saying' warns the listener before the honesty arrives. It prepares the listener and can soften what follows, but the content and tone still have to be respectful. _Examples:_ I hope you don't mind me saying this. / I hope you don't mind, but it needs work.
- **p2 — with respect**: 'With respect' signals disagreement with someone senior or in public. In British English it can sound sharp, so the tone matters. _Examples:_ With respect, I see it differently. / With respect, the figures say otherwise.

## 64. Would You Be So Kind  `b2-would-you-be-so-kind`

**Type:** phrases · **Focus:** formal requests and how far up the scale to go · **Role play targets:** would you be so kind / I wonder if you could / if it's not too much
